\chapter{Background}
\label{chap:background}

This chapter gives the technical background the rest of the thesis depends on: the end-to-end driving setting, the NAVSIM benchmark and its score, the visual backbones swapped in the experiments, and the four planners in which those backbones are evaluated. The literature survey is in Chapter~\ref{chap:relatedwork}.

\section{End-to-End Autonomous Driving}
\label{sec:bg-e2e}

End-to-end driving learns a direct mapping from raw sensor observations to future vehicle motion~\cite{bojarski-pilotnet-2016,chen-e2e-survey-2024}. The model consumes images, LiDAR, and the ego state, and predicts a sequence of future waypoints. This differs from modular pipelines that decompose the task into perception, prediction, and planning with explicit intermediate interfaces~\cite{caesar-nuscenes-2020,karnchanachari-nuplan-2024,hu-uniad-2023,jiang-vad-2023}. The end-to-end formulation lets the representation optimise jointly against the driving objective; the cost is that internal representations are harder to interpret and failures under distribution shift are harder to diagnose~\cite{codevilla-limitations-2019,zhai-rethinking-2023}.

The failure mode this thesis focuses on is geographic. A planner that interpolates well on a pooled four-city benchmark may still fail when deployed in an unseen city. The experiments in Chapter~\ref{chap:experiments} show that this failure is strongly mediated by the visual backbone. Comparing backbones under identical planners is what the rest of this chapter is set up to support.

\subsection{Imitation Learning for Trajectory Prediction}
\label{sec:bg-il}

Most modern end-to-end planners are trained with behavioural cloning~\cite{bojarski-pilotnet-2016,ross-dagger-2011}. Given expert demonstrations $\{(o_t, a_t^\ast)\}$, the policy $\pi_\theta(a_t \mid o_t)$ is trained to reproduce the expert action. In NAVSIM the observation $o_t$ comprises an eight-camera surround view, a merged LiDAR point cloud, a history of ego kinematic states, and a route descriptor; the action is eight future poses sampled at $2$~Hz over a four-second horizon~\cite{dauner-navsim-2024,chitta-transfuser-2023}. Training is open-loop: the model predicts a trajectory from logged sensor data and is supervised against the recorded future motion. Open-loop displacement error is an incomplete proxy for driving quality because many trajectories can be acceptable in the same scene and because small errors compound under closed-loop rollout~\cite{ross-dagger-2011,zhai-rethinking-2023,dauner-pdm-2023}. NAVSIM addresses this by scoring the predicted plan against an explicit rule set that includes safety, legality, and comfort.

\subsection{The NAVSIM Benchmark}
\label{sec:bg-navsim}

NAVSIM is a data-driven planning benchmark constructed on top of real-world nuPlan and OpenScene logs \cite{dauner-navsim-2024,karnchanachari-nuplan-2024,openscene-2023}. Each scenario provides eight surround-view camera images, a merged LiDAR point cloud assembled from the vehicle's roof-mounted and side-mounted sensors, a history of ego-state values including position, velocity, acceleration, and yaw rate, a future route defined as a sequence of reference points on the lane graph, and the logged future trajectory of the human driver. Scenarios are drawn from four cities: Boston, Pittsburgh, and Las Vegas in the United States, and Singapore, which uses left-hand driving. The standard splits are \texttt{navtrain} with approximately $103{,}000$ scenarios and \texttt{navtest} with approximately $12{,}000$ scenarios, both filtered from the underlying nuPlan logs to emphasize challenging driving situations such as intersections, lane changes, unprotected turns, and interactions with other road users \cite{dauner-navsim-2024}. A separate \texttt{navhard\_two\_stage} split is used for the extended evaluation introduced in NAVSIM v2. It is harder by construction because it draws on scenarios that are more difficult to plan even for the reference simulator planner, and because it is paired with the reactive two-stage evaluation protocol described below \cite{navsim-v2-2024}.

The headline metric is the Predictive Driver Model Score, PDMS, which aggregates five sub-scores into a single scalar \cite{dauner-pdm-2023,dauner-navsim-2024}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pdms}
\mathrm{PDMS} \;=\; \underbrace{\prod_{m \in \mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{mul}}} s_m}_{\text{multiplicative terms}} \;\times\; \underbrace{\frac{\sum_{m \in \mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{avg}}} w_m \, s_m}{\sum_{m \in \mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{avg}}} w_m}}_{\text{weighted average}},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{M}_\text{mul}$ and $\mathcal{M}_\text{avg}$ are defined in \reftab{tab:bg-pdms-subscores}. NAVSIM v2 extends the aggregation to EPDMS by adding four further compliance and comfort terms (DDC, TLC, LK, EC)~\cite{navsim-v2-2024}. Because the multiplicative safety terms dominate, a single at-fault collision or a single drivable-area violation is sufficient to drive PDMS to zero on that scenario, which makes the metric sensitive to rare but critical failures rather than only to typical-case accuracy.

NAVSIM supports two evaluation modes. In the non-reactive mode used for the default \texttt{navtest} leaderboard, the predicted trajectory is rolled out against a log-replay world in which background agents follow their recorded future motion, and PDMS is computed from the resulting simulated rollout. In the reactive two-stage mode introduced with NAVSIM v2, background vehicles are replayed for a short horizon and then handed over to an IDM-based reactive policy so that their behavior can respond to the ego plan rather than being fixed ahead of time \cite{navsim-v2-2024,dauner-pdm-2023}. The two-stage protocol exposes planning failures that non-reactive evaluation cannot, because it requires the predicted plan to remain valid under modest perturbations of the surrounding agents. The schematic in Figure~\ref{fig:bg-navsim-pipeline} summarizes this evaluation flow and the relationship between PDMS and its sub-scores.

One aspect of NAVSIM is central to this thesis. The benchmark does not natively expose per-city train/test splits. Under the default protocol, data from Boston, Pittsburgh, Las Vegas, and Singapore are pooled in both training and evaluation, which means standard validation primarily measures interpolation inside the combined distribution. The city-aware split construction introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology} is therefore a structural precondition for the experiments that follow, because it is the construction that makes geographic robustness measurable at all.

\subsection{Open-Loop Evaluation on nuScenes}
\label{sec:bg-nuscenes-ol}

The open-loop leg of this thesis uses nuScenes~\cite{caesar-nuscenes-2020}, which contains data from Boston (right-hand driving) and Singapore (left-hand driving) and is the standard open-loop cross-city benchmark in prior work~\cite{zhai-rethinking-2023,li-law-2024}. Predicted trajectories are scored against the logged future motion using L2 displacement and a collision rate derived from object-level ground truth. Throughout the thesis, open-loop nuScenes results are cited by L2 and collision rate, and closed-loop NAVSIM results are cited by PDMS.

\section{Supervised and Self-Supervised Visual Backbones}
\label{sec:bg-backbones}

The manipulated variable throughout the thesis is the visual backbone. Two supervised baseline families are used. ResNet-34~\cite{he-resnet-2016} is the default image encoder of TransFuser, Latent TransFuser, and DiffusionDrive. Swin-Transformer-Tiny~\cite{liu-swin-2021} is the default image encoder of LAW. Both are ImageNet-pretrained~\cite{deng-imagenet-2009}, and both represent the supervised state of practice that the SSL variants in this thesis are compared against. Using two supervised defaults rather than one reduces the risk that an observed SSL advantage is an artefact of a specific supervised architecture.

\subsection{Vision Transformers (ViT)}
\label{sec:bg-vit}

Vision Transformers replace convolution with patch tokenisation and global self-attention~\cite{dosovitskiy-vit-2021}. An image of resolution $H \times W$ is divided into patches of side length $P$, linearly projected to tokens of dimension $D$, and processed by a stack of $L$ transformer blocks (multi-head self-attention followed by a feed-forward network, with residual connections and layer normalisation)~\cite{vaswani-attention-2017}. The output representation is read from the \texttt{CLS} token or by mean-pooling the patch tokens. Figure~\ref{fig:bg-vit} shows the pipeline.

ViT is the only architecture on which the three SSL objectives studied here (I-JEPA, DINOv2, MAE) can be compared on equal footing. Model capacity is parameterised by patch size $P$, embedding dimension $D$, and depth $L$. The standard configurations used are ViT-S/14 ($P{=}14$, $D{=}384$, $L{=}12$), ViT-B/16 ($P{=}16$, $D{=}768$, $L{=}12$), and ViT-H/14 ($P{=}14$, $D{=}1280$, $L{=}32$).

\section{Self-Supervised Learning for Vision}
\label{sec:bg-ssl}

Self-supervised learning produces visual representations from images alone by defining a pretext task whose supervision is extracted from the input~\cite{lecun-ssl-path-2022,balestriero-ssl-cookbook-2023}. This thesis compares three representative objectives: I-JEPA (predictive, latent space)~\cite{assran-ijepa-2023}, DINOv2 (self-distillation)~\cite{oquab-dinov2-2024,caron-dino-2021}, and MAE (generative, pixel reconstruction)~\cite{he-mae-2022}. Contrastive methods such as SimCLR~\cite{chen-simclr-2020} and MoCo~\cite{he-moco-2020} are not evaluated directly. The three objectives do not learn the same invariances, and that difference is what the cross-city study tests.

\subsection{Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (I-JEPA)}
\label{sec:bg-ijepa}

I-JEPA predicts the representation of masked image regions from visible context rather than reconstructing pixels~\cite{assran-ijepa-2023}. It has three components. A context encoder $f_\theta$ processes visible patches, a target encoder $f_{\bar\theta}$ (maintained as an exponential moving average of $f_\theta$) processes masked target regions, and a predictor $g_\phi$ maps the context representation to the target representation conditioned on target position. The loss is MSE in representation space:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ijepa-loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{I-JEPA}} \;=\; \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M} \bigl\| g_\phi\bigl(f_\theta(x_c),\, p_i\bigr) - \mathrm{sg}\bigl[f_{\bar\theta}(x_{t_i})\bigr] \bigr\|_2^2,
\end{equation}
where $x_c$ is the context, $x_{t_i}$ the $i$-th target block, $p_i$ its spatial position, and $\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$ the stop-gradient on the target branch. Targets are four large blocks per image (each $15$--$20\%$ of image area), which biases the pretext task toward semantic rather than local-pixel prediction. Latent-space supervision, patch-token operation on ViT backbones, and the absence of hand-crafted augmentations are the three design choices that differentiate I-JEPA from DINOv2 and MAE. The architecture is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bg-ijepa}.

Two variants of I-JEPA appear in the experiments: the ImageNet-pretrained ViT-H/14 release from Assran et al.~\cite{assran-ijepa-2023}, used off the shelf at full capacity, and a set of nuScenes-pretrained ViT-S/14 checkpoints produced under a controlled recipe (Section~\ref{sec:meth-ssl-pretraining}). The contrast between the two separates pretraining scale from pretraining domain in Chapter~\ref{chap:experiments}.

\subsection{DINOv2}
\label{sec:bg-dinov2}

DINOv2 trains a student network to match the output distribution of a momentum teacher under augmented views of the same image~\cite{oquab-dinov2-2024,caron-dino-2021}. Both project to a probability distribution over a learnable prototype codebook, and the student is trained with cross-entropy against the teacher. Centering and sharpening of the teacher output avoid the collapse solution. DINOv2 scales this recipe to large curated data (LVD-142M) and produces features that transfer competitively across classification, retrieval, and dense prediction. In this thesis DINOv2 is the non-predictive SSL baseline: if DINOv2 and I-JEPA both improve on supervised pretraining under cross-city transfer, the gain is shared across SSL rather than specific to the predictive objective.

\subsection{Masked Autoencoders (MAE)}
\label{sec:bg-mae}

MAE~\cite{he-mae-2022} is an asymmetric encoder-decoder that masks $75\%$ of image patches, passes only the visible patches through a ViT encoder, and uses a lightweight decoder to reconstruct the masked pixels under an L2 loss on normalised pixels. Only the encoder is retained for downstream use. Unlike I-JEPA and DINOv2, MAE's loss is on pixels, so it must encode fine appearance detail. This is useful as a diagnostic, because it tests whether SSL gains come from the pretraining objective or from pretraining scale alone.

\subsection{Comparison of SSL Paradigms}
\label{sec:bg-ssl-compare}

The three objectives encourage different invariances. I-JEPA supervises in latent space and biases toward semantic structure. DINOv2 optimises for global image identity under augmentation and produces strong off-the-shelf transfer features. MAE supervises on pixels and preserves fine appearance detail. Chapter~\ref{chap:experiments} tests whether these differences matter once the model is evaluated under city-disjoint transfer.

\section{Planning Architectures}
\label{sec:bg-planning}

The thesis evaluates visual backbones inside four planners, each of which plays a distinct diagnostic role. TransFuser and Latent TransFuser are the closed-loop NAVSIM references. A perception-free MLP head is the minimum-capacity test of whether a backbone alone drives performance. LAW provides a stable open-loop setting in which only the image encoder changes. DiffusionDrive is the generative planning head. Holding planner identity fixed while varying the backbone is what makes the representation question tractable.

\subsection{TransFuser}
\label{sec:bg-transfuser}

TransFuser~\cite{chitta-transfuser-2023,prakash-transfuser-2021} fuses camera and LiDAR features through transformer-style cross-modal attention and regresses a future trajectory with a GRU waypoint head conditioned on ego status and a driving command. Both streams use a ResNet-34 backbone in the NAVSIM configuration. Published TransFuser and Latent TransFuser means on pooled \texttt{navtest} are $83.9 \pm 0.4$ and $83.5 \pm 0.6$ PDMS respectively~\cite{dauner-navsim-2024}; the full reference recipe is reproduced in Appendix~\ref{app:hparams}. The architecture is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bg-transfuser}.

Latent TransFuser (LatTF) is the camera-only variant: the LiDAR input is replaced by a fixed learned latent, and the rest of the fusion stack is preserved~\cite{chitta-transfuser-2023,dauner-navsim-2024}. LatTF is important in the cross-city setting because the TransFuser LiDAR branch can overfit to city-specific geometry on small single-city partitions, and removing it acts as an implicit regulariser.

\subsection{Lightweight Perception-Free Heads}
\label{sec:bg-lightweight-heads}

The lightweight regime pairs a pretrained visual encoder with a minimal planning head: either an MLP waypoint regressor or a small transformer decoder over image tokens and ego state, with no LiDAR input and no auxiliary perception task. The purpose is diagnostic clarity. If a frozen SSL backbone improves performance even when the planner is deliberately simple, the improvement is attributable to the representation. Drive-JEPA~\cite{wang-drive-jepa} makes a related point: strong JEPA-style pretraining makes lightweight decoders competitive on pooled NAVSIM.

\subsection{Latent World Model (LAW)}
\label{sec:bg-law}

LAW is an external open-loop planner~\cite{li-law-2024}, used in this thesis as a baseline; it is not claimed as an original contribution. At time $t$, the encoder produces a latent $z_t$, a world-model predictor forecasts the next-step latent $\hat{z}_{t+1}$, and a planner head regresses a waypoint sequence from $z_t$. The world-model objective shapes the encoder toward temporally predictable features, while the planner is supervised against the logged human trajectory. LAW's default image encoder is Swin-T pretrained on ImageNet~\cite{liu-swin-2021}; in this thesis's cross-city benchmark, that Swin-T encoder is replaced by each of the pretrained ViTs above, while the predictor and the planner head are held fixed. Using LAW alongside TransFuser spans two different supervised backbone families: if the SSL advantage persists across both defaults, the effect is less likely to be a backbone-architecture artefact. The structure is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bg-law}.

\subsection{Truncated Diffusion Planning (DiffusionDrive)}
\label{sec:bg-diffusiondrive}

DiffusionDrive~\cite{jia-diffusiondrive-2025} belongs to a different planning family. It refines trajectory proposals through a truncated diffusion process initialised from a bank of $K$-means anchor trajectories clustered on expert motions. Only two denoising steps are used at inference, which retains multi-modal expressiveness while remaining real-time. The scene features that condition the denoising come from a backbone-adapter pair; in the default configuration, a ResNet-34 produces a spatial feature map, and a cross-attention conditioner injects it into every denoising block. DiffusionDrive matters here because its head is generative and is designed to attenuate backbone contributions; it is the strictest substrate on which to test whether the representation-centric findings survive a change of head. The pipeline is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bg-diffusiondrive}.

\section{Domain Generalization in Autonomous Driving}
\label{sec:bg-domain-gen}

Domain generalization asks whether a model trained on one distribution remains effective on unseen but related domains without further adaptation~\cite{wang-domain-gen-survey-2022,zhou-domain-gen-survey-2023}. The zero-shot variant is the strictest form: a model trained on $\mathcal{D}_s$ is evaluated on $\mathcal{D}_t \neq \mathcal{D}_s$ with no fine-tuning, no test-time adaptation, and no access to target-domain samples during training. Most driving-specific work focuses on appearance shifts such as weather and lighting~\cite{sakaridis-acdc-2021,pan-cycada-2018,dosovitskiy-carla-2017}. Geographic transfer is broader: it changes lane structure, intersection topology, road markings, traffic density, vehicle fleet composition, and in some cases driving convention. This thesis studies the problem at the planning layer rather than at the perception layer, because the deployment failure mode that matters operationally is a failed manoeuvre, not a missed bounding box.

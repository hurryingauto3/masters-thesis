These references establish two points used throughout the rest of the chapter. First, the benchmark is non-trivial: ego-state prediction alone is insufficient. The thesis question is therefore more specific than which planner scores highest in aggregate. It is which parts of that performance survive under geographic transfer.

\section{Exploratory Lightweight-Head Study}
\label{sec:exp-phase0}

This thesis did not begin with city splits. The original working hypothesis was simpler: if representation quality is genuinely central to end-to-end driving, then replacing a supervised image backbone with a self-supervised one should already help even when the planner head is intentionally weak. The exploratory regime of~\ref{sec:meth-lightweight-head} operationalizes this hypothesis through the \texttt{LightweightPlanningAgent}: eleven backbones, two adaptation modes, the same MLP head, the same training recipe, the same 20-epoch budget on the pooled \texttt{navtest} split. Its purpose is not leaderboard ambition but causal cleanliness.

\subsection{Findings}
\label{sec:exp-phase0-findings}

Seven observations follow from the Priority-1, Priority-3, and Cross-City results above.

\paragraph{Frozen I-JEPA ViT-H/14 is the strongest minimum-head configuration.} The top row of Table~\ref{tab:exp-phase0-p1} remains competitive even against most fully-unfrozen small-ViT rows in Table~\ref{tab:exp-phase0-p3}. This is the strongest single argument for frozen self-supervised features plus a lightweight adapter: the representation carries enough planning-relevant structure that backbone gradients do not have to do most of the work.

\paragraph{MAE leads the fully-fine-tuned leaderboard; I-JEPA leads the frozen one.} The ranking flips between Tables~\ref{tab:exp-phase0-p1} and~\ref{tab:exp-phase0-p3}: I-JEPA is strongest when frozen, while MAE is strongest after full fine-tuning. The reconstructive objective of MAE appears to produce features that adapt best under end-to-end fine-tuning, while I-JEPA's latent-predictive features are more usable off-the-shelf.

\paragraph{Self-supervised pretraining beats supervised ResNet-34 under the same head.} In both adaptation modes, most SSL variants exceed the ResNet-34 baseline row highlighted in gray. The SSL rows that trail are architectural edge cases rather than the typical deployment setting.

\paragraph{Fine-tuning lift depends on the backbone.} Table~\ref{tab:exp-phase0-lift} shows that adaptation lift is not uniform across families. Small ViTs and the supervised ResNet baseline benefit most, while I-JEPA ViT-H/14 and the rectangular I-JEPA ViT-S/14 row move least. The latter is treated as a known-flaky configuration because it unfreezes EMA target-encoder weights together with non-square positional embeddings.

\paragraph{Rectangular pretraining does not help pooled in-domain PDMS.} For both MAE and I-JEPA, the square ViT-S/14 variant outperforms its rectangular counterpart on pooled \texttt{navtest} in both adaptation modes. DINOv2 is tied within $0.1$ percentage points. This replicates the observation in our earlier benchmark study~\cite{naeinian-cross-city-2026} that the rectangular-input advantage is specific to explicit cross-city evaluation and does not appear on pooled in-domain splits, and it motivates the inversion of the comparison in~\ref{sec:exp-phase1}.

\paragraph{Cross-city ranking follows the pooled ranking but amplifies the SSL gap.} The mean row of Table~\ref{tab:exp-phase0-cc} shows the same ordering as the pooled study, but with a larger separation between the strongest SSL backbones and the supervised baseline. This is the exploratory preview of the main thesis claim: representation choice matters more once the evaluation distribution is a different city from the training distribution.

\paragraph{Singapore is the cross-city anomaly.} The Singapore row of Table~\ref{tab:exp-phase0-cc} compresses the spread across all four backbones. Singapore is the smallest source domain and the only left-hand-traffic city, so the representation advantage is partially eaten by limited downstream gradient signal. This matches the directional-asymmetry finding in our earlier cross-city study~\cite{naeinian-cross-city-2026}.

\section{Cross-City SSL Benchmark}
\label{sec:exp-phase1}

This section synthesizes the core cross-city benchmark reported in our earlier study~\cite{naeinian-cross-city-2026}. It establishes the empirical foundation for the thesis and explains the pivot away from pure mixed-city performance. Once geographic mixing is removed from evaluation, robustness depends much more strongly on the visual representation than on the downstream planning head.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
The phase-1 protocol has two complementary parts. In open-loop evaluation, LAW is trained on nuScenes under strict Boston/Singapore geographic splits and evaluated in both in-domain and cross-city directions. In closed-loop evaluation, TransFuser and Latent TransFuser are trained on single cities in NAVSIM and evaluated on the remaining three cities. Across both settings, the downstream planner is held fixed while the backbone varies across supervised baselines, generic ImageNet-pretrained SSL models, and domain-specific nuScenes-pretrained I-JEPA, DINOv2, and MAE variants. Frozen and fully fine-tuned downstream settings are both included, which makes it possible to separate the value of the pretrained representation from the value of downstream adaptation~\cite{naeinian-cross-city-2026}.

\subsection{In-Distribution Performance}
\label{sec:exp-phase1-indist}

The in-distribution story is intentionally unspectacular. \reftab{tab:exp-law-indist} shows that the supervised and SSL LAW variants occupy the same broad range on the standard mixed-city nuScenes split~\cite{naeinian-cross-city-2026}. This is the pattern that motivates the rest of the chapter: mixed-city validation suggests that most competent backbones are roughly comparable, so it hides the degree to which some of them are learning city-specific shortcuts.

\subsection{Cross-City Transfer Matrices}
\label{sec:exp-phase1-matrix}

The transfer matrices reveal structure that is invisible in the pooled metrics. On nuScenes, the Boston$\rightarrow$Singapore direction is consistently harder than Singapore$\rightarrow$Boston, especially for supervised or weakly adapted backbones. On NAVSIM, the full four-city matrix shows the same phenomenon at larger scale: Boston is generally a stronger training city than Singapore for single-city transfer, while Singapore is the harshest test environment because it combines the smallest dataset with the only left-hand-traffic regime~\cite{naeinian-cross-city-2026}. The resulting pattern answers RQ2 in a practical way: the ``best'' source city is the one that exposes the model to structural diversity without overfitting it to idiosyncratic local cues.

\subsection{Transfer Asymmetry Analysis}
\label{sec:exp-phase1-asymmetry}

Transfer asymmetry is not noise; it is one of the most important findings of the benchmark. The hardest direction is typically from a right-hand-traffic city into Singapore, not the reverse. That asymmetry is consistent with a representational explanation: a backbone trained only on right-hand-traffic scenes can overfit to layout priors that become actively misleading in Singapore, whereas a model trained in Singapore is forced to encode some structure that remains useful when moved back to right-hand-traffic cities. Dataset scale compounds the effect. Singapore is also the smallest source domain in NAVSIM, so a model trained there has fewer opportunities to learn a diverse but reusable representation~\cite{naeinian-cross-city-2026}.

\section{SSL Backbones in Generative Planning}
\label{sec:exp-phase2}

The generative-planning study revisits in-distribution NAVSIM under a planner that is designed to attenuate backbone contributions: DiffusionDrive~\cite{jia-diffusiondrive-2025}. Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}, Section~\ref{sec:meth-dd-why} develops the rationale in full; the short form is that DiffusionDrive denoises trajectories from K-means anchor initializations in two steps, conditions through grid-sampled BEV cross-attention, and uses time-only FiLM, and the composite effect is a head that can produce good trajectories even when the image features are weak. This makes DiffusionDrive a demanding test of the representation claim. The goal of this study is therefore not to top an in-distribution leaderboard; it is to measure whether any backbone-dependent structure survives DiffusionDrive's attenuation, because that is the harder precondition for the cross-city generative study in \refsec{sec:exp-phase3}. The ViT adapter is held identical to the one used in the cross-city benchmark (both pipelines import \texttt{create\_image\_encoder} from the same TransFuser backbone module), so the planner head is the only manipulated variable between the two studies.

\subsection{SSL vs.\ Supervised in Generative Planning}
\label{sec:exp-phase2-ssl-vs-sup}

The all-city rows in Table~\ref{tab:diffusiondrive-allcity} mirror the cross-city benchmark's in-distribution pattern: SSL is slightly weaker than the supervised ResNet-34 baseline under pooled evaluation. That observation is important because it prevents an overly simple story. If mixed-city PDMS were the only question, stronger planner design could dominate the narrative; SSL becomes interesting because later studies ask whether that small in-distribution cost is repaid under domain shift.

\subsection{Camera-Only: SSL Under a LiDAR-Free Generative Head}
\label{sec:exp-phase2-camera-only}

The camera-only rows in Table~\ref{tab:diffusiondrive-allcity} characterise what a LiDAR-free DiffusionDrive sees when the image encoder is varied. The supervised ResNet-34 baseline (A13) reaches $86.6$~PDMS, which is only $1.3$~points below the corresponding camera$+$LiDAR configuration (A12, $87.9$~PDMS). Among the completed camera-only SSL variants, rectangular frozen I-JEPA ViT-S/14 (A13-h) is the strongest at $85.8$~PDMS, followed by square frozen MAE (A13-b, $83.5$), square frozen DINOv2 (A13-l, $81.5$), and square trainable I-JEPA (A13-j, $76.1$). On the completed evidence, no camera-only SSL row exceeds the camera-only supervised baseline and none recovers the full camera$+$LiDAR score.

This is a useful negative control. Under DiffusionDrive, changing the image backbone does not replace the geometric signal removed with LiDAR. The camera-only block therefore serves as a bound on what the visual encoder alone can recover inside the same generative head. The cross-city extension of this matrix (single-city training under the LiDAR-free head with the same SSL backbones) was not run; \refsec{sec:exp-phase3-limitations} states the consequence for the cross-city claim.

\subsection{Frozen vs.\ Fine-Tuned Backbone}
\label{sec:exp-phase2-frozen}

Within DiffusionDrive, the I-JEPA rows in Table~\ref{tab:diffusiondrive-allcity} show that preprocessing matters at least as much as fine-tuning. The simplest interpretation is that matching the ViT pretraining geometry is more important than unfreezing the backbone on the mixed-city split. This fits the broader thesis argument that frozen SSL weights act as a form of implicit regularization: they preserve pretrained invariances while still allowing the adapter and planner to specialize downstream.

The Singapore-trained row is the cleanest single piece of evidence in the matrix. Singapore is the smallest of the four training cities (\refsec{app:city-data-card}), and \texttt{Rn34-sin} produces a marked overfit: in-distribution PDMS is $54.3$, mean OOD is $39.4$, and the gap is $14.9$ points. Frozen I-JEPA on the same Singapore data behaves qualitatively differently. Its in-distribution PDMS is $47.6$, its mean OOD is $47.9$, and the in-distribution-to-mean-OOD drop is $-0.3$ points. The model trained exclusively on the smallest training city generalizes to held-out cities at least as well as it does to its own training city. DINOv2 and MAE Singapore-trained rows show drops of $11.7$ and $6.1$ points, both substantially smaller than the supervised $14.9$. Across the SSL family, training on the smallest single city does not reproduce the supervised collapse: the pretrained representation generalizes despite the small training-data regime. This is the most direct evidence in the thesis for the claim that frozen SSL representations resist single-city overfitting under generative planning.

The Vegas-trained row is the opposite extreme. Vegas contributes the largest training pool, $818$ logs against Singapore's $97$ (\refsec{app:city-data-card}), and yet the four Vegas-trained $\Delta_\text{OOD}$ values are the largest in the entire matrix: $28.3$ for ResNet-34, $29.8$ for I-JEPA, $27.5$ for DINOv2, and $29.9$ for MAE. Every backbone overfits to Vegas, in the same direction and to within $2.4$ points of the same magnitude. Under the same DiffusionDrive head and the same training schedule, abundant single-city data therefore does not narrow the cross-city drop. The simplest reading is that Vegas's large training pool lets every backbone reach the planner's single-city representational ceiling, after which the head's anchor prior dominates the output and the visible across-backbone variation collapses. The Singapore-trained and Vegas-trained rows together bound the operating regime: at one extreme, training-data scarcity collapses supervised baselines but not frozen SSL; at the other extreme, training-data abundance saturates every backbone equally on the training city while still failing to bridge geographic shift. Across both extremes, the operating lever for cross-city DiffusionDrive performance is the representation, not the volume of single-city training data.

The matched-train-city paired comparison on Singapore (right panel of \reffig{fig:dd-cc-full}) extends the directional reading of \reftab{tab:dd-cc-criterion} into the most informative destination column. Across the nine pairs in which the training city is not Singapore, seven SSL pairs produce a smaller Singapore drop than the matched ResNet-34 baseline. The largest single-cell improvement is DINOv2 trained on Pittsburgh: a $5.2$-point smaller Singapore drop than ResNet-34 trained on Pittsburgh, while the in-distribution Pittsburgh PDMS differ by only $1.4$ points. The smallest improvement among the seven winning cells is $0.3$ for MAE on Vegas; the two losses are $0.5$ for I-JEPA-bos and $1.7$ for MAE-bos. The signal is broad rather than carried by any single (backbone, training-city) pair: DINOv2 nuScenes is the consistent winner across every training city, while I-JEPA and MAE show the same direction in the strongest cells.

The per-metric breakdown in Appendix~\ref{app:per-metric} traces the gain to drivable-area compliance. Across the twelve (Rn34, SSL, training-city, Singapore) cells with a non-Singapore training city, the Singapore-destination drop is concentrated in DAC and EP, while NC and TTC remain comparatively stable. Within the Pittsburgh-trained Singapore-eval cells, frozen DINOv2 raises DAC from $61.7$ to $68.0$ ($+6.3$ points) and frozen I-JEPA raises it to $64.1$ ($+2.4$). NC stays within $1.2$ points across all four backbones in that group and TTC within $0.9$. The cross-city DiffusionDrive gain is therefore attributable to better scene-geometry compliance under shift, not to safer reactive driving.

The expected attenuation of the SSL gain across planners is also visible. Under Latent TransFuser, nuScenes-pretrained MAE improves average OOD PDMS by more than four points across Boston-, Pittsburgh-, and Las Vegas-trained models~\cite{naeinian-cross-city-2026}. The analogous comparison under DiffusionDrive averages an SSL gain of $2.20$ to $2.84$ points in mean OOD PDMS relative to ResNet-34, depending on the SSL family (I-JEPA $+2.84$, MAE $+2.38$, DINOv2 $+2.20$). The DiffusionDrive head therefore compresses the SSL OOD-PDMS gain to roughly half to two-thirds of its Latent TransFuser size. This is exactly what \refsec{sec:meth-dd-why} predicted and what the table-by-table reading in this section consistently shows. The compression does not reverse the ordering, and the residual gain is large enough to clear the pre-registered criterion in \reftab{tab:dd-cc-criterion}. Reading the cross-city DiffusionDrive matrix as a stress test, the attenuating planner attenuates as expected; the representation effect survives.

The cross-city DiffusionDrive evidence is therefore load-bearing rather than supportive. The pre-registered criterion is met, the per-backbone aggregate ordering matches the cross-city benchmark in \refsec{sec:exp-phase1}, and the failure-mode decomposition is consistent with the representation-level mechanism developed in \refsec{sec:exp-why-ssl}. The remaining caveat in \refsec{sec:exp-phase3-limitations} is the single I-JEPA camera-only DiffusionDrive configuration that was not trained, which is acknowledged in the limitations rather than re-engineered around.

\subsection{Limitations of the Cross-City Generative Study}
\label{sec:exp-phase3-limitations}

Two limitations of the cross-city DiffusionDrive matrix should be flagged explicitly. First, the $20$-epoch single-city schedule produces absolute PDMS that is markedly below the all-city DiffusionDrive references in \reftab{tab:diffusiondrive-allcity}. This is a budget choice: the single-city training pool (as small as $\sim 9{,}000$ scenes for the Singapore row) reaches the schedule's effective capacity well before the full $100$-epoch DiffusionDrive recipe would. The diagonal cells are therefore not directly comparable to the all-city table; what is comparable, by design, is the off-diagonal structure that this study isolates. Second, one originally specified configuration, frozen I-JEPA ViT-S/14 nuScenes rectangular under the camera-only LiDAR-free DiffusionDrive variant, was not trained. The other camera-only rows in \reftab{tab:diffusiondrive-allcity} stand, but the matched cross-city camera-only matrix is therefore camera+LiDAR only. The thesis does not claim a camera-only cross-city result.

\subsection{Representation Similarity Across Cities (CKA)}
\label{sec:exp-cka}

Linear CKA is defined on two representations of the same samples, so we use the cross-dataset form that compares feature second-moment structures. For cities $A$ and $B$ with centered feature matrices $X_A$ and $X_B$, we report
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CKA}(X_A, X_B)
=
\frac{\langle X_A^\top X_A,\; X_B^\top X_B \rangle_F}
     {\lVert X_A^\top X_A \rVert_F \,\lVert X_B^\top X_B \rVert_F},
\end{equation}
which is bounded in $[0, 1]$, invariant to orthogonal rotations of the feature basis, and agnostic to different sample counts across cities.

\reffig{fig:exp-cka-concept} gives the intuition. Two cities yield two feature matrices $X_A \in \mathbb{R}^{N_A \times d}$ and $X_B \in \mathbb{R}^{N_B \times d}$ with different sample counts. The cross-dataset form compares their $d \times d$ feature-covariance matrices $X_A^\top X_A$ and $X_B^\top X_B$ rather than their raw feature vectors. Linear CKA is therefore a similarity between the directions that carry variance in each city, not between the feature values themselves.

\subsection{Feature Distribution Shift (MMD)}
\label{sec:exp-mmd}

We compute biased RBF-kernel MMD\textsuperscript{2} between city-pair feature distributions with a median-heuristic bandwidth $\sigma^2$ estimated from a shared 500-sample pool, clamping all squared distances at 0 to avoid numerical blow-up on near-duplicate samples (observed on a handful of trainable single-city DINOv2 runs pre-clamp). Absolute MMD\textsuperscript{2} values are not directly comparable across backbones because $\sigma^2$ differs; within-regime comparisons remain informative. Fig.~\ref{fig:mmd-vs-ood} is the pooled scatter; the Boston-only cluster reproduces the single-city ordering (Table~\ref{tab:probe-boston}).

\reffig{fig:exp-mmd-concept} gives the intuition for the two non-obvious choices. The bandwidth $\sigma^2$ is set to the median of the pairwise squared distances in a shared pool of features, which fixes the kernel scale to a quantity the data itself defines. The zero-clamp on squared distances prevents a handful of near-duplicate frames (two video frames a fraction of a second apart) from producing a negative squared distance under floating-point rounding, which would then blow up the exponent.

\subsection{Feature-Covariance Rank (Implicit Regularization)}
\label{sec:exp-implicit-reg}

The original plan was to measure the effective rank of learned planning-head weights. With frozen SSL backbones the planning head is the only drift vector, which would make it a clean test; however, for supervised ResNet-34 the backbone itself is trainable, so planning-head rank alone cannot be compared fairly. We instead report the effective rank of the \emph{feature covariance} for each (run, city) pair: per-city features are centered, $\sigma_i$ denotes the $i$-th singular value of the centered matrix, and we report $\exp\!\bigl(-\sum_i (\tilde\sigma_i^2) \log (\tilde\sigma_i^2)\bigr)$ with $\tilde\sigma_i$ the $\ell_2$-normalized singular values~\cite{roy-effrank-2007}. This probes representational structure rather than downstream-head capacity.

\reffig{fig:exp-rank-concept} gives three toy spectra and their effective rank values to make the formula readable without the entropy notation. The effective rank is the exponential of the Shannon entropy of the $\ell_2$-normalised squared singular values, so it collapses the spectrum to a single number that measures how spread the feature variance is across directions. A spectrum concentrated on one direction has effective rank close to $1$; a uniform spectrum across $k$ directions has effective rank close to $k$; an exponentially decaying spectrum sits in between.

\subsection{Synthesis: A Mechanistic Account}
\label{sec:exp-why-synthesis}

The five analyses converge on three related findings.

First, trainable supervised backbones drift toward city-discriminative cues. The probe protocol and aggregate values are in Tables~\ref{tab:exp-probe-protocol} and~\ref{tab:family-aggregate}; the matched Boston comparison is in Table~\ref{tab:probe-boston}. This is the representation-mechanism claim of the thesis, restricted to the cell in which it is identifiable.

Second, the pooled probe-vs-drop correlation is weak because training-city confounds dominate and because the MAE family is both city-decodable and robust. The pretraining objective therefore matters beyond its effect on probe accuracy: the masked-autoencoder objective preserves scene content in a way that can remain useful under transfer.

Third, the frozen constraint is itself load-bearing. The effective-rank rows in Table~\ref{tab:family-aggregate} and the collapse cases in Table~\ref{tab:exp-rank-collapse} show that fine-tuning can compress SSL feature subspaces enough to erase useful variation. Preserving the pretrained rank structure is, empirically, what keeps the SSL representation useful for transfer.

Two cautions matter for committee-facing interpretation. CKA is descriptive rather than decisive: it shows that feature-covariance structure is broadly aligned across cities, but it does not by itself explain why one family transfers better than another. MMD is a sanity check rather than a predictor: absolute values depend on the backbone-specific kernel scale and are only informative within matched comparisons. The load-bearing mechanistic evidence is therefore the matched probe ordering and the frozen-vs-trainable rank contrast, not the pooled global correlations.

The confounders are also explicit. Training-city scale and traffic convention clearly affect the raw transfer matrices; Singapore is both the smallest training source and the only left-hand-traffic city. Pretraining domain and backbone capacity matter as well: domain-specific nuScenes pretraining can help independently of the SSL objective, and ViT-H versus ViT-S comparisons are not capacity matched. For that reason, the strongest causal claims in this thesis are restricted to matched settings where those variables are held fixed, and the full-roster summaries are treated as synthesis rather than as a single pooled law.

Cross-city robustness in this study is therefore best summarized as three claims:
(i) supervised backbones learn city identity almost for free, and the fraction they learn predicts their transfer deficit within matched comparisons;
(ii) SSL objectives differ materially, with MAE leading the OOD rankings and DINOv2 most prone to collapse under fine-tune;
(iii) the operational lever with the highest leverage is not the SSL objective alone but whether the encoder is frozen at inference time.

\section{Ablation Studies}
\label{sec:exp-ablations}

\subsection{Input Resolution: Rectangular vs.\ Square Crop}
One ablation that is already informative from the generative-planning study is input geometry. For I-JEPA in DiffusionDrive, the square-crop frozen configuration (86.3 PDMS) outperforms the rectangular frozen configuration (85.2 PDMS). This suggests that matching the input geometry of ViT pretraining can matter more than preserving the raw driving aspect ratio, at least when the downstream adapter is lightweight.

\subsection{Frozen vs.\ Fine-Tuned Backbone (Extended)}
The frozen-versus-fine-tuned question remains central across studies. Available evidence from the generative-planning study and the mechanistic analysis suggests that fine-tuning can slightly improve in-distribution fit, but also risks eroding the pretrained invariances that make SSL useful under transfer. The feature-rank collapse observed in fully trainable I-JEPA provides a concrete mechanistic warning against unconstrained fine-tuning on modest driving datasets.

\subsection{Pre-Training Data: ImageNet vs.\ nuScenes}
The ImageNet-versus-driving-data comparison is addressed most directly in the cross-city benchmark, where domain-specific nuScenes pretraining consistently yields the most stable cross-city results. The lesson is not that generic pretraining is useless, but that domain alignment matters much more under geographic transfer than it does on mixed-city validation.

\subsection{ViT Scale: ViT-S vs.\ ViT-H}
Backbone scale is a practical trade-off rather than a purely academic one. The larger ViT-H variants are useful for diagnosing transfer in the cross-city benchmark, while smaller ViT-S variants are more realistic inside DiffusionDrive where throughput matters. The broader pattern in this thesis is that objective and freezing strategy matter more reliably for robustness than raw backbone size alone.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:exp-discussion}

Taken together, the studies support a single thesis argument. The exploratory pilots showed that SSL could already make lightweight planners competitive, which justified treating representation quality as a controlled variable rather than as a background implementation choice. The cross-city benchmark then made the key point visible: once evaluation is city-disjoint, backbone choice dominates robustness. The generative-planning study showed that this representation-centric story survives a major planner change in-distribution, and the mechanistic analysis explained why: frozen SSL features are less city-discriminative and occupy a more stable feature subspace than supervised or fully fine-tuned alternatives.

The practical recommendation is therefore asymmetric. If the target deployment environment is well matched to the training mix, the strongest supervised in-distribution planner may be adequate. If deployment includes genuinely new cities, the better trade-off is usually to accept a small in-distribution cost in exchange for much smaller geographic failure modes. The main limitations of the current evidence are the restriction to four cities, the heavy reliance on benchmark proxy evaluation, and the fact that the cross-city DiffusionDrive matrix is only partially complete. Even so, the completed DiffusionDrive rows already point in the same direction as the main benchmark: Singapore remains the hardest destination and the frozen SSL backbone loses less performance on the matched Pittsburgh transfer.

\paragraph{Industrial implications.}
The main industrial implication is operational rather than architectural. A fleet that is expanded city by city cannot assume that strong pooled-benchmark performance will survive deployment in a new geography. The completed TF and LatTF matrices show that this assumption is unsafe, especially for Singapore-like shifts. In that setting, a frozen SSL backbone is attractive because it trades a small in-distribution cost for a more stable transfer profile without requiring expensive city-specific relabelling or a full planner redesign. The camera-only DiffusionDrive results also speak to deployment cost: better visual representations help lower-cost sensing stacks, but they do not fully recover the geometric signal removed with LiDAR. For industrial benchmarking, the broader lesson is that pooled leaderboard numbers are insufficient. Geographic holdout evaluation should be part of any serious validation protocol for end-to-end driving.

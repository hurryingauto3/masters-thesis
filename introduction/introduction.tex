\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

End-to-end autonomous driving learns a single visual-to-motion stack, avoiding hand-engineered interfaces between perception, prediction, and planning. Under the standard pooled evaluation protocol, the paradigm appears mature: recent planners evaluated on NAVSIM report strong aggregate PDMS and match or exceed classical modular baselines on geographically mixed test sets~\cite{dauner-navsim-2024}. The protocol itself is the caveat. A model that interpolates well across pooled data from Boston, Pittsburgh, Las Vegas, and Singapore is not necessarily robust to deployment in a city it has not seen.

Once evaluation is re-framed as zero-shot geographic transfer, the picture changes. Naeinian et al.~\cite{naeinian-cross-city-2026} report order-of-magnitude open-loop degradation for supervised Boston-to-Singapore transfer, while closed-loop NAVSIM results show smaller out-of-distribution drops for self-supervised features. Chapter~\ref{chap:experiments} reports the exact matrices and ratios. The implication is that mixed-city validation does not expose the failure mode that matters for deployment in a new city.

This thesis studies how visual-representation choice mediates these failures. The central claim is bounded rather than absolute:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Under explicit city-disjoint evaluation, end-to-end driving failures are strongly mediated by the visual representation. In the evaluated settings, frozen self-supervised backbones transfer more reliably across cities than supervised baselines, while planner choice appears secondary to representation choice.}
\end{quote}

\noindent
The scope of this claim is deliberately tied to the evaluated settings (\reftab{tab:intro-rqs}). The thesis does not claim that planner architecture is irrelevant, nor that self-supervised features are city-invariant by construction; it defends a narrower and more defensible version of the representation-over-architecture argument on the evidence currently completed.

\section{Scope and Framing}
\label{sec:project-evolution}

The thesis began with a narrower question: whether a frozen self-supervised encoder paired with a small MLP waypoint regressor could already improve NAVSIM planning under mixed-city evaluation~\cite{hamza-sanchez-navsim-report}. Drive-JEPA~\cite{wang-drive-jepa} subsequently showed that V-JEPA-style video pre-training is already highly competitive on pooled NAVSIM, making mixed-city PDMS an insufficient discriminator for a representation-focused thesis. The scope shifted from \emph{whether} SSL helps mixed-city planning to \emph{where} it matters most and \emph{why}, with geographic transfer identified as the regime in which representation choice carries the most diagnostic weight.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}

The subject of study is geographic domain shift in end-to-end planning. The cities used in this thesis differ in road topology, lane geometry, infrastructure layout, traffic density, and driving convention (right-hand traffic in Boston, Pittsburgh, and Las Vegas; left-hand traffic in Singapore). These structural differences create a harder transfer setting than mixed-city validation, because city-specific shortcuts learned during training do not transfer~\cite{naeinian-cross-city-2026}.

The thesis isolates the \textbf{representation axis} as its primary axis: holding the planner, training recipe, and evaluation protocol fixed, we vary the visual backbone across supervised and self-supervised initialisations and measure cross-city behaviour. A secondary \textbf{planner-generalization analysis} asks whether the representation signal observed under regression-based planners (LAW, TransFuser, Latent TransFuser) also survives under a generative planner designed to attenuate backbone differences (DiffusionDrive). Both portions of this analysis are completed and reported in Chapter~\ref{chap:experiments}: the mixed-city integration in \refsec{sec:exp-phase2} and the city-disjoint $4 \times 4 \times 4$ matrix in \refsec{sec:exp-phase3}. The pre-registered criterion in \refsec{sec:meth-dd-success} is met on the latter, so the cross-city DiffusionDrive evidence is part of the defended result.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

The thesis contributions are completed contributions. Prior-work boundaries are stated explicitly and locally, so the original contribution is easy for a reader to identify.

\paragraph{Provenance of external components.}
Two classes of prior-work components are used inside the study and are named here once so that later chapters do not need to repeat the disclaimer. The LAW open-loop planner~\cite{li-law-2024} is an external baseline; it is included because it lets the representation question be tested on a stable open-loop planner alongside the closed-loop NAVSIM planners, and its world-model head is neither claimed nor modified. Several backbone initialisations (I-JEPA ViT-H/14 on ImageNet, DINOv2, MAE, ResNet-34 supervised) use pretrained checkpoints released in prior work; the contribution of this thesis is their systematic evaluation under city-disjoint closed-loop transfer, not the pretrained weights themselves. Domain-specific nuScenes SSL pretraining was carried out by Fatemeh Naeinian as part of our companion cross-city study~\cite{naeinian-cross-city-2026}; the thesis documents her recipe in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology} for completeness. Everything else is original to this thesis: the city-disjoint evaluation framing, the representation-centred investigation, the NAVSIM v1.1 re-evaluation, the TF and LatTF reproductions, the lightweight-head studies, the DiffusionDrive integration, and the mechanistic analysis and synthesis.

\paragraph{Completed contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Cross-city evaluation framework}. A per-city train/test partitioning of NAVSIM across Boston, Pittsburgh, Las Vegas, and Singapore, together with the paired open-loop (nuScenes) and closed-loop (NAVSIM) city-disjoint protocol. The partition first appears in our earlier cross-city study~\cite{naeinian-cross-city-2026}; this thesis integrates it into a broader representation-centred investigation and adds a NAVSIM v1.1 re-evaluation that hardens the numbers against devkit-version confounding.
    \item \textbf{Cross-city representation benchmark}. A controlled evaluation of self-supervised backbones (I-JEPA, DINOv2, MAE) against supervised ResNet-34 and Swin-T baselines, using LAW on nuScenes and TransFuser / Latent TransFuser on NAVSIM as fixed planners. Under the evaluated settings, frozen self-supervised backbones reduce cross-city degradation relative to supervised baselines; the matrices and ratios are in Chapter~\ref{chap:experiments}.
    \item \textbf{DiffusionDrive integration (mixed-city and cross-city)}. Self-supervised backbones are integrated into the DiffusionDrive generative head through the same feature adapter used in the cross-city benchmark, including mixed-city camera-only rows. The mixed-city integration shows that the representation signal survives a planner swap under pooled evaluation at a small in-distribution cost relative to the supervised camera$+$LiDAR baseline. The completed $4 \times 4 \times 4$ city-disjoint camera$+$LiDAR matrix shows that the representation signal also survives under city shift: every frozen self-supervised family produces a smaller mean out-of-distribution PDMS drop than supervised ResNet-34 under a planner whose anchor-based head is designed to attenuate backbone variation, and the per-training-city criterion in \refsec{sec:meth-dd-success} is met.
    \item \textbf{Mechanistic analysis}. A study of the trained checkpoint roster using linear probing for city identity, centred kernel alignment, maximum mean discrepancy, and feature-covariance effective rank. In matched and training-city-stratified comparisons, the linear-probe ordering tracks the out-of-distribution PDMS drop ordering, and fine-tuning compresses feature-covariance rank for every self-supervised family. CKA and MMD are supporting diagnostics rather than decisive mechanisms, and no claim is made that the mechanism holds universally across checkpoints.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Thesis Organization}
\label{sec:organization}

The remainder of the thesis is organised as follows.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:background}} covers the technical background needed to read the experiments: end-to-end driving, NAVSIM, the self-supervised objectives evaluated (I-JEPA, DINOv2, MAE), and the planner families used later in the thesis.
    \item \textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:relatedwork}} reviews the surrounding literature in self-supervised learning for driving, cross-city generalisation, and diffusion-based planning, and explains why Drive-JEPA moved pooled NAVSIM performance out of the center of the thesis question.
    \item \textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}} follows the study sequence in \reffig{fig:intro-study-map}: first the lightweight-head regime, then the TransFuser / Latent TransFuser cross-city benchmark, then DiffusionDrive as a second anchor planner, and finally the mechanistic tools used to interpret the gains.
    \item \textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:experiments}} reports the results in the same order: lightweight-head results, TransFuser / Latent TransFuser cross-city results, DiffusionDrive results, mechanistic evaluation, and a discussion section that interprets the practical implications.
    \item \textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusions}} closes with the thesis contributions, key findings, industrial implications, limitations, and future work.
\end{itemize}

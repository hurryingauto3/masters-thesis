\section{Exploratory Lightweight-Head Regime}
\label{sec:meth-lightweight-head}

The project began with an intentionally simple experimental regime: pair a pretrained visual backbone with a lightweight planning head and ask whether the representation alone already improves end-to-end driving behavior. This regime is deliberately minimal. By holding planner capacity fixed at its lowest meaningful level and varying only the backbone, any change in downstream performance is attributable to the representation rather than to the planner. The chapter continues from this starting point; understanding the lightweight regime is therefore a prerequisite for the cross-city experiments in~\ref{sec:meth-city-splits} and the generative-head studies in Sections~\ref{sec:meth-diffusiondrive}--\ref{sec:meth-phase3}.

\subsection{Role in the Thesis}
\label{sec:meth-lh-role}

Three questions motivated the exploratory regime. First, whether the in-distribution improvement of SSL over supervised backbones reported in related work~\cite{wang-drive-jepa, naeinian-cross-city-2026} survives when the planner is stripped to a waypoint regressor. Second, whether different SSL objectives (I-JEPA, DINOv2, MAE) separate from one another under the same head, or whether their differences require a heavier planner to become visible. Third, whether fine-tuning the backbone helps or hurts relative to freezing it, once the planning head is this small. The answers to those questions set the prior for every subsequent planner choice in the thesis.

The answer from Drive-JEPA~\cite{wang-drive-jepa} reframed the scope. Their result that V-JEPA plus a lightweight decoder is already competitive on standard NAVSIM argued that in-distribution PDMS on the pooled benchmark is close to saturation for SSL methods. The exploratory regime therefore serves less as a competitive submission and more as a controlled baseline: it produces the head-fixed, backbone-varying numbers against which the cross-city and diffusion-head studies measure their own contributions.

Every block in \reffig{fig:method-lh-arch} is addressable by configuration. The camera set is selected by a \texttt{camera\_mode} field that resolves to one, three, or six nuPlan views. The backbone is constructed by the same \texttt{create\_image\_encoder} factory used in the TransFuser and DiffusionDrive variants of~\ref{sec:meth-backbones}; it accepts any backbone in \reftab{tab:backbones} and exposes an \texttt{unfreeze\_last\_blocks} hook for encoder-fine-tune ablations. The view-fusion module $f_\text{view}$ consumes a $(B, V, N, C)$ token tensor and returns a $(B, C)$ summary; five implementations are available. The head consumes $[\text{visual} \;\|\; \text{ego}]$ and emits eight poses. Keeping the interface narrow at each seam lets the experiment matrix vary one factor at a time.

Two decisions that cut across the axes in \reftab{tab:meth-lh-design} are worth calling out explicitly. First, when \texttt{trainable\_backbone\_fraction}${=}\,1.0$, the optimizer places encoder parameters in a separate group with a lower learning rate than the head, matching the two-group AdamW schedule used in~\ref{sec:meth-phase1-law}. Priority~1 is the core scientific instrument of this section. Each of the backbones listed in \reftab{tab:backbones} is paired with the same frozen-encoder \texttt{mlp} head over a single front camera, trained for the same 20 epochs, and evaluated on the pooled \texttt{navtest} split. The only variable is the backbone identity. Priority~2 extends the protocol across the four nuPlan cities introduced in~\ref{sec:meth-city-splits} to produce a first cross-city readout at minimal planner capacity, and adds the \texttt{mean}, \texttt{cross\_attn}, and \texttt{weighted\_mean} fusion ablations on a fixed I-JEPA backbone to isolate the fusion axis. \subsection{From Lightweight Head to Cross-City}
\label{sec:meth-lh-bridge}

Three observations carry forward from this regime. The lightweight \texttt{mlp} head is sufficient to discriminate backbones: with planner capacity held at its minimum, backbone choice alone moves in-distribution PDMS by several points, as reported in~\ref{sec:exp-phase0}. This means the cross-city study in the following sections can use a similarly minimal planner without risking that the reported gaps are masked by planner expressivity. The fusion interface $(B, V, N, C) \to (B, C)$ generalizes to the heavier planners later in the chapter, so the same multi-camera variants can be revisited without rewriting the agent. Finally, the same \texttt{LightweightHeadConfig} dataclass drives all 45 runs; this is what makes it practical to extend the matrix with four training cities in~\ref{sec:meth-city-splits} without a corresponding code expansion.

The remaining open question, and the one~\ref{sec:meth-city-splits} takes up, is whether the backbone ordering produced by this regime on a pooled benchmark survives geographic distribution shift, or whether SSL's apparent advantage collapses once train and test cities differ. That question cannot be answered at the level of \texttt{navtest}; it requires a re-partitioning of the benchmark along city identity, which the next section builds.

\section{NAVSIM City Splits}
\label{sec:meth-city-splits}

\subsection{City Extraction from nuPlan Metadata}
\label{sec:meth-city-extraction}

City labels are recovered directly from the underlying nuPlan/OpenScene log metadata. Each scenario token inherits the city prefix of its source log, with prefixes such as \texttt{us-ma-boston}, \texttt{us-nv-las-vegas}, \texttt{us-pa-pittsburgh}, and \texttt{sg-one-north}. A preprocessing script walks the metadata once, assigns every scenario token to exactly one city, and writes city-specific token lists used by the training and evaluation dataloaders. This step is simple but essential: it turns NAVSIM from a pooled benchmark into a reproducible geographic-transfer benchmark whose train/test partitions are defined by city identity rather than random sampling.

\subsection{Split Statistics and Distribution}
\label{sec:meth-split-stats}

The cross-city protocol uses the natural geographic partitions already present in the source datasets. For nuScenes~\cite{caesar-nuscenes-2020}, the Boston split contains 390 training scenes (2.12 hours) and 77 validation scenes (0.42 hours), while Singapore contains 310 training scenes (1.68 hours) and 73 validation scenes (0.40 hours). For NAVSIM~\cite{dauner-navsim-2024}, the city distribution is more imbalanced: Las Vegas contributes 818 training scenes and 50 evaluation scenes, Boston 255 and 62, Pittsburgh 140 and 22, and Singapore 97 and 13. These counts are large enough to expose meaningful transfer behavior while still making the data imbalance itself part of the problem; Singapore is both the smallest domain and the only left-hand-traffic environment, which makes it a particularly stringent test of transfer~\cite{naeinian-cross-city-2026}. \reffig{fig:method-city-map} illustrates the four cities schematically alongside their train/test scene counts; the full data card is in \refapp{app:city-data-card}.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocols}
\label{sec:meth-eval-protocols}

The thesis uses a hierarchy of evaluation settings. The standard all-city protocol serves as a reference point: train and evaluate on the benchmark's original mixed-city split. The key stress test is strict single-city transfer, where a model is trained on one city and evaluated on unseen cities without fine-tuning, adaptation, or test-time modification. On nuScenes this yields a two-direction protocol (Boston$\rightarrow$Singapore and Singapore$\rightarrow$Boston); on NAVSIM it yields a full $4 \times 4$ train-city/test-city matrix. We also describe leave-one-city-out evaluation as a useful future extension, but the core empirical results in this thesis come from the first two settings because they match the controlled protocol introduced in~\cite{naeinian-cross-city-2026}.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{All-city (standard)}: Train and test on the full NAVSIM split (all cities mixed). Reproduces published numbers.
    \item \textbf{Single-city (zero-shot transfer)}: Train on city A, test on all cities. Produces a $4 \times 4$ generalization matrix.
    \item \textbf{Leave-one-city-out (LOCO)}: Train on 3 cities, test on the held-out city. More realistic multi-city regime.
\end{enumerate}

For score-based metrics such as PDMS, we summarize out-of-distribution performance for a training city $c_i$ by averaging over the other cities~\cite{naeinian-cross-city-2026}:
\begin{equation}
    S_\text{OOD}(c_i)=\frac{1}{|C|-1}\sum_{c_j \neq c_i} S(c_i \rightarrow c_j).
\end{equation}

We additionally report the generalization gap as the difference between in-domain and out-of-domain closed-loop performance:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_\text{gen} = \text{PDMS}_\text{in-dist} - \text{PDMS}_\text{OOD}.
\end{equation}

For open-loop error metrics, where lower is better, the more informative quantity is the error inflation ratio
\begin{equation}
    R_\text{err}(c_\text{train}\rightarrow c_\text{test})=\frac{E_\text{cross}(c_\text{train}\rightarrow c_\text{test})}{E_\text{in}(c_\text{train}\rightarrow c_\text{train})},
\end{equation}
which equals 1 for perfect preservation and grows as transfer degrades~\cite{naeinian-cross-city-2026}.

\section{SSL Pre-Training Protocol}
\label{sec:meth-ssl-pretraining}

All domain-specific SSL pretraining used in this thesis was carried out by Fatemeh Naeinian, first author of our earlier cross-city benchmark study~\cite{naeinian-cross-city-2026}, on a shared pretraining repository maintained for this collaboration. This section documents the recipe she followed, which is the protocol under which every nuScenes-pretrained ViT-S/14 backbone in Chapters~\ref{chap:methodology} and~\ref{chap:experiments} was produced. The goal of the protocol is not to maximize absolute benchmark performance during pretraining; it is to create matched representation initializations whose downstream behavior can be compared fairly under city transfer. Holding the architecture, data, schedule, and input resolution fixed across three SSL families is what turns the backbone comparisons in Chapter~\ref{chap:experiments} into a controlled intervention rather than an uncontrolled family comparison.

\subsection{Common Setup: Dataset, Architecture, and Input Geometry}
\label{sec:meth-ssl-common}

The three pretraining recipes share a common substrate. The pretraining source is the nuScenes training split~\cite{caesar-nuscenes-2020}, expanded to all six camera rigs (\texttt{CAM\_FRONT}, \texttt{CAM\_FRONT\_LEFT}, \texttt{CAM\_FRONT\_RIGHT}, \texttt{CAM\_BACK}, \texttt{CAM\_BACK\_LEFT}, \texttt{CAM\_BACK\_RIGHT}) rather than the front camera alone. This yields \(168{,}780\) RGB frames. Using every camera matters for two reasons. It increases the pretraining signal roughly six-fold relative to a front-only protocol, and it exposes the encoder to the full driving field of view that downstream planners will see, including side and rear cameras whose statistics differ from front-facing ImageNet pretraining. All frames are normalized with the ImageNet RGB mean and standard deviation; no colour jitter, Gaussian blur, or heavy photometric augmentation is applied, because such augmentations bias the invariance set of the representation toward classification tasks and are not load-bearing for driving planning.

The model architecture is ViT-S/14~\cite{dosovitskiy-vit-2021}: patch size \(14\), embedding dimension \(384\), depth \(12\), \(6\) heads. ViT-S/14 is deliberately smaller than the off-the-shelf ImageNet baselines used in the comparison (I-JEPA ViT-H/14, DINOv2 ViT-L/14, MAE ViT-L/16), because the domain-specific runs are compute-bounded on a single H200 and because the relevant question is whether driving-domain pretraining at a smaller scale can match or exceed a much larger generic pretraining under geographic transfer.

The input geometry is a controlled variable. Each of the three SSL objectives is pretrained in two resolutions: a canonical \emph{square} \(224 \times 224\) crop that mirrors ImageNet convention, and a \emph{rectangular} \(224 \times 392\) crop whose aspect ratio matches the native driving cameras more closely. A rectangular pretraining ingests horizontal context (sidewalks, adjacent lanes, oncoming traffic) that a square crop discards; a square pretraining is cheaper and compatible with a larger pool of released ViT checkpoints. Both variants are trained to completion so that the subsequent transfer study can disentangle the contribution of the SSL objective from the contribution of input geometry. \reftab{tab:meth-ssl-common} summarizes the substrate.

\subsection{I-JEPA: Predictive Latent Pretraining}
\label{sec:meth-ssl-ijepa}

The I-JEPA implementation is a fork of the official Meta release~\cite{assran-ijepa-2023}, vendored into the pretraining repository and adapted to consume the nuScenes pickle instead of ImageNet. The objective is exactly the one introduced in Section~\ref{sec:bg-ijepa}: a context encoder $f_\theta$ processes a visible context block covering \(85\)--\(100\%\) of the image; a predictor $g_\phi$ uses the context features to predict the latent representations that a target encoder $f_{\bar\theta}$ computes for four target blocks, each covering \(15\)--\(20\%\) of the image, at aspect ratios sampled uniformly from \([0.75, 1.5]\). Target masking is spatially coherent rather than random per-patch, which biases the pretext task toward semantic layout. The loss is the MSE in latent space, and no pixel-level reconstruction path exists.

The target encoder $f_{\bar\theta}$ is an exponential moving average of $f_\theta$ with a momentum scheduled linearly from \(0.996\) at iteration zero to \(1.0\) at the end of training. The optimizer is AdamW with base learning rate \(1 \times 10^{-3}\), warmup to \(2 \times 10^{-4}\) over 40 epochs, weight decay scheduled linearly from \(0.04\) to \(0.4\), and a final learning rate of \(1 \times 10^{-6}\). Checkpoints are serialized as \texttt{.pth.tar} files with keys \texttt{encoder}, \texttt{target\_encoder}, and \texttt{predictor}; downstream consumers load the \texttt{target\_encoder} weights, which match the published convention in~\cite{assran-ijepa-2023}.

\subsection{DINOv2: Self-Distillation Pretraining}
\label{sec:meth-ssl-dinov2}

The DINOv2 implementation is a fork of the official Meta release~\cite{oquab-dinov2-2024}, modified to accept the nuScenes pickle via a \texttt{NuScenesFromPKL} wrapper and to fix the patch size to \(14\) (the stock release uses patch \(16\)) so that it matches the I-JEPA and MAE configurations. The objective is student--teacher self-distillation with multi-crop augmentation: two global crops at the native resolution and six local crops at \(96 \times 96\) are extracted per image; the student encoder processes all crops and the teacher encoder processes only the global ones; the student is trained to match the teacher's softmax-normalized prototype distribution with temperature \(0.1\) (student) and \(0.04\) (teacher). Patch-level iBOT loss is disabled in this configuration because its pretraining benefit does not transfer cleanly to the small driving corpus. The teacher is an EMA of the student at base momentum \(0.9985\).

Optimization is AdamW at base learning rate \(2 \times 10^{-3}\) (single-GPU scale; the canonical multi-GPU recipe uses \(4 \times 10^{-3}\)), with a 10-epoch linear warmup, cosine decay, weight decay \(0.04 \to 0.4\), gradient clipping at \(3.0\), and FP16 mixed precision. Batch size is \(768\) per H200. Checkpoints are serialized as \texttt{.pth} with a \texttt{student} sub-dictionary (wrapped by \texttt{MultiCropWrapper}) and a \texttt{teacher} sub-dictionary; downstream transfer extracts the teacher backbone state.

\subsection{MAE: Masked Reconstruction Pretraining}
\label{sec:meth-ssl-mae}

The MAE implementation is a fork of the official Meta release~\cite{he-mae-2022}, with a \texttt{Masked\-Autoencoder\-ViT} variant whose patch embedding and positional embeddings are adapted to the \(224 \times 392\) rectangular input. The objective is the canonical masked-patch pixel-reconstruction task. For each image, \(75\%\) of the patches are masked uniformly at random; the encoder processes only the visible patches; a lightweight decoder (depth \(8\), embedding \(512\), \(16\) heads) reconstructs the pixel values at the masked positions under an MSE loss. No EMA teacher is involved because the MAE objective is pixel-level and single-pathway.

The optimizer is AdamW at base learning rate \(1.5 \times 10^{-4}\) with the standard linear scaling by effective batch size, cosine decay to a minimum learning rate of zero, a 40-epoch linear warmup, and weight decay \(0.05\). Batch size is \(768\) per H200; training uses 16 dataloader workers with pinned memory and a prefetch factor of 4. Checkpoints are serialized as \texttt{.pth} with a single \texttt{model} sub-dictionary; downstream transfer loads the encoder portion.

\subsection{Rectangular vs.\ Square Inputs}
\label{sec:meth-ssl-rect}

The rectangular-input variants exist because the three cross-city camera splits in Chapter~\ref{chap:experiments} are captured at native aspect ratios that are closer to \(16{:}9\) than to \(1{:}1\). Cropping driving frames to square either discards \(42\%\) of the horizontal field of view (if the crop is centered) or introduces a strong centering bias (if the crop is randomized). Both are representationally costly. The \(224 \times 392\) rectangular variant is therefore the configuration that actually matches the downstream planners' input; the \(224 \times 224\) square variant is kept as a control because it allows a like-for-like comparison with ImageNet-pretrained ViT checkpoints and because prior work~\cite{wang-drive-jepa} pretrained square.

Implementing rectangular inputs requires two adjustments that the SSL libraries do not support out of the box. First, the patch embedding module is rewritten to accept a non-square grid: for \(224 \times 392\) at patch size \(14\), the patch grid is \(16 \times 28\) rather than \(16 \times 16\). Second, the positional embedding is regenerated at the rectangular grid rather than bicubically interpolated from a square pretraining, which preserves the exact \((x, y)\) prior rather than inheriting an anisotropically-warped one. Both adjustments land in the rectangular forks (\texttt{ijepa-rect/}, \texttt{dinov2-rect/}, \texttt{mae-rect/}) and are documented in~\cite{naeinian-cross-city-2026}. The downstream transfer code in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology} consumes both variants through the same factory (Section~\ref{sec:meth-backbones}); the \texttt{vit\_backend}${=}\,\texttt{custom}$ path is the one that handles exact positional embeddings for rectangular pretrained checkpoints.

\label{sec:meth-ssl-imagenet}

\subsection{Backbone Adapter Interface}
\label{sec:meth-backbone-adapter}

\section{Zero-Shot Cross-City Transfer}
\label{sec:meth-phase1-cc}

The cross-city benchmark is the controlled representation-versus-geography study introduced in our earlier cross-city work~\cite{naeinian-cross-city-2026}, extended here with the full set of backbone configurations from~\ref{sec:meth-ssl-pretraining} and integrated through the same factory described in~\ref{sec:meth-backbones}. The question it answers is narrow and well-posed: holding the planner and training recipe fixed, does the choice of visual backbone change how a model behaves when it is trained in one city and evaluated in a different one? The protocol is split across two planner families --- an open-loop latent-world-model planner on nuScenes~\cite{caesar-nuscenes-2020} and closed-loop multi-modal / camera-only planners on NAVSIM~\cite{dauner-navsim-2024} --- so that the finding is not contingent on a single planner's inductive bias.

The collaboration split of labour matters for attribution. The open-loop LAW pipeline was implemented and run by Fatemeh Naeinian (first author of~\cite{naeinian-cross-city-2026}); it is described here only to the extent needed to interpret its numbers. The closed-loop NAVSIM pipeline, including the TransFuser / Latent TransFuser backbone-swap infrastructure and the configuration-hardening work that these variants required, is the author's contribution and is treated at more detail.

\subsection{Open-Loop Pipeline: LAW on nuScenes}
\label{sec:meth-phase1-law}

The open-loop leg uses LAW~\cite{li-law-2024} with its stock Swin-Transformer-Tiny image encoder replaced by each of the pretrained ViTs described in~\ref{sec:meth-backbones}. The planner --- per-view spatial decoders, LID depth encoding, delta-waypoint regression, and the auxiliary latent world-model head --- is held fixed across backbone variants; the only manipulated factor is the encoder initialization. This design follows~\cite{naeinian-cross-city-2026}. \reftab{tab:meth-phase1-train} summarizes the training recipe.

\subsection{Closed-Loop Pipeline: TransFuser and Latent TransFuser on NAVSIM}
\label{sec:meth-phase1-tf}

The closed-loop leg evaluates the same representational question under NAVSIM's pseudo-simulation scorer, where the predicted trajectory is rolled out against log-replay agents and PDMS is computed from the resulting rollout (Section~\ref{sec:bg-navsim}). Two architectures are used side by side. \emph{TransFuser}~\cite{chitta-transfuser-2023} retains its original ResNet-34 LiDAR branch but replaces the ResNet-34 image branch with the SSL-pretrained encoder under test; the cross-modal fusion stack is unchanged. \emph{Latent TransFuser} is the same model with the LiDAR branch removed and replaced by a fixed learned latent, as in~\cite{dauner-navsim-2024}; it serves as the camera-only probe and is the critical variant for testing whether the representation effect survives without the geometric-sensing shortcut. Running both in the same table isolates the contribution of LiDAR from the contribution of the backbone.

Plugging a ViT encoder into a fusion stack that was designed around a CNN feature pyramid is not a one-line change. The image encoder must produce a four-stage pyramid whose channel dimensions match what the fusion stack consumes; the adapter used here is the one described in~\ref{sec:meth-feature-proj}. Position embeddings are regenerated at the native camera-crop grid (per the \texttt{vit\_backend}${=}\,\texttt{custom}$ path) rather than bicubically interpolated from a square pretraining, which matters for the rectangular nuScenes-pretrained ViT-S/14 variants. None of these changes alter the fusion layers, the waypoint decoder, or the training loss.

The single non-trivial methodological note in this leg is a faithfulness issue specific to TransFuser. The original NAVSIM TransFuser recipe~\cite{dauner-navsim-2024} uses plain Adam at a constant learning rate of $10^{-4}$, zero weight decay, no scheduler or warmup, gradient clipping at L2 norm 1.0, an effective batch size of 128, and 100-epoch training for the full baseline (20 epochs for cheaper runs). Several defaults in modern Lightning / Hydra scaffolding (cosine schedulers, AdamW with non-zero decay, mismatched LiDAR-branch depth, accumulator double-counting) silently degrade this recipe by tens of PDMS points if imported unchanged. The NAVSIM v1.1 re-evaluation sidecar used later in the thesis preserves this recipe verbatim; the configuration is listed in \reftab{tab:meth-phase1-train}.

\subsection{Generalization-Gap Metrics}
\label{sec:meth-phase1-metrics}

The in-domain to cross-city behavior is reduced to two scalar summaries that appear throughout Chapter~\ref{chap:experiments}; both follow~\cite{naeinian-cross-city-2026}.

For open-loop error metrics (L2 displacement and collision rate on nuScenes), where lower is better, the transfer inflation from training city \(c_\text{train}\) to evaluation city \(c_\text{test}\) is reported as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:meth-rerr}
R_\text{err}(c_\text{train} \to c_\text{test})
=
\frac{E_\text{cross}(c_\text{train} \to c_\text{test})}
     {E_\text{in}(c_\text{train} \to c_\text{train})},
\end{equation}
so that \(R_\text{err}=1\) indicates no degradation under transfer and \(R_\text{err}>1\) indicates degradation. Ratios are reported separately for L2 displacement and for collision rate.

For closed-loop score metrics (PDMS on NAVSIM), where higher is better, we report the mean out-of-distribution PDMS of a training city,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:meth-sood}
S_\text{OOD}(c_i)
=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}|-1}
\sum_{c_j \neq c_i} S(c_i \to c_j),
\end{equation}
which averages the closed-loop scores across every unseen test city. \(S_\text{OOD}\) reduces the \(4\times 4\) NAVSIM matrix to one scalar per training city per backbone, which is the quantity plotted in the per-city panels of Chapter~\ref{chap:experiments}.

Two practical notes. First, \(S_\text{OOD}\) averages over a heterogeneous set of test cities; it is not symmetric in the sense that training on a hard source and evaluating on easy targets can give the same mean as the reverse, so it is always reported alongside the full matrix rather than in isolation. Second, the ratio \(R_\text{err}\) is sensitive to the denominator: when the in-domain run itself has a degenerate score, the ratio becomes uninformative, and such runs are flagged in the tables of Chapter~\ref{chap:experiments} rather than silently included in ranked comparisons.

\section{Generative Planning with DiffusionDrive: a Hostile-to-Backbone Test}
\label{sec:meth-diffusiondrive}

The cross-city benchmark establishes that the visual representation dominates planner capacity under geographic transfer, but it does so under two planner families --- LAW and TransFuser / Latent TransFuser --- whose heads are both regression-based and whose cross-modal fusion is a thin attention stack over a convolutional feature pyramid. A natural follow-up question is whether the representation-centric finding is specific to planners with low expressive capacity, or whether it also holds under a planner whose own priors are strong enough to do most of the work. DiffusionDrive~\cite{jia-diffusiondrive-2025} was selected for this test precisely because its head is closer to the second category.

\subsection{Why DiffusionDrive is the Right Adversarial Planner}
\label{sec:meth-dd-why}

DiffusionDrive's planner absorbs backbone differences by construction. Four of its design choices make this explicit:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{K-means anchor trajectories.} Denoising is initialized from one of 256 prototypical trajectories obtained by K-means clustering on \texttt{navtrain} expert motions~\cite{jia-diffusiondrive-2025}. The planner therefore \emph{starts from a plausible trajectory} on every scenario, rather than from Gaussian noise. A weak backbone whose features cannot resolve fine-grained scene geometry can still exit the denoiser close to the expert trajectory, because the anchor has already placed the plan in the right basin.
    \item \textbf{Truncated two-step denoising.} At inference, DiffusionDrive takes only two denoising steps. The visual conditioning therefore has two opportunities to perturb the anchor before commitment. This is a design decision for real-time throughput~\cite{jia-diffusiondrive-2025}, but its side effect is that each individual conditioning step contributes less than it would under a 50- or 1{,}000-step denoiser.
    \item \textbf{Grid-sampled BEV cross-attention at waypoint positions.} The cross-attention that injects scene features into the denoising query is evaluated on a regular BEV grid around the current waypoint prediction, not on a dense feature map. This quantizes the planner's access to the backbone features and makes the head robust to differences in token geometry.
    \item \textbf{Time-only FiLM conditioning.} The final conditioning path uses FiLM modulation on the denoising timestep only. Backbone-dependent modulations that were explored during development did not end up in the production path; the head therefore sees the backbone features only through the grid-sampled cross-attention, with no additional FiLM gate that might amplify them.
\end{enumerate}

Each of these choices is known from the DiffusionDrive paper~\cite{jia-diffusiondrive-2025} and is preserved verbatim in this thesis. The composite effect is that DiffusionDrive is a \emph{hostile substrate for testing the representation claim}: if a different backbone changes planner behavior at all under this head, that change must overcome the anchor-trajectory prior, the two-step budget, and the quantized cross-attention. A planner whose head was already doing most of the work under DiffusionDrive would read as an \emph{absence} of backbone effect on this planner, and that would be the cleanest way for the thesis claim to fail.

The consequence for interpretation is that small absolute PDMS differences under DiffusionDrive are more meaningful than similarly-sized differences under a weaker planner. Specifically, the study reports transfer-ratio inflation (in-distribution PDMS divided by out-of-distribution PDMS) rather than raw absolute scores. A ResNet-34 baseline that drops 16 points to Singapore and an SSL variant that drops 14 points is a directionally consistent robustness signal even though the absolute gap is small, because the DiffusionDrive head is actively attenuating the backbone contribution before the PDMS scorer sees the plan.

\subsection{Integration: Same Adapter as the Cross-City Benchmark}
\label{sec:meth-dd-ssl}

The central repro-fairness commitment of the generative-planning study is that the SSL backbone is plugged into DiffusionDrive through the \emph{same} adapter used in the cross-city benchmark. In code, both pipelines import \texttt{create\_image\_encoder} from the TransFuser backbone module (\ref{sec:meth-backbones}); DiffusionDrive's image-encoder configuration is a drop-in replacement at the same interface. There is no bespoke DiffusionDrive-specific ViT adapter, no resolution-specific branch divergence, and no "feature pyramid up-sampling" patch that is applied here but not in the cross-city benchmark.

This matters because it forecloses the obvious alternative explanation for any observed effect: "maybe DiffusionDrive sees the backbone more richly because its adapter is different." The adapter is held fixed, and the head is the single manipulated variable between the cross-city benchmark and this study. An ablation branch (\texttt{fix/vit-architecture-bottlenecks}) explores a set of ViT-specific resolution-matching commits that could, in principle, further narrow the backbone--planner gap; those commits are \emph{not} merged into the thesis's main branch and \emph{not} applied to any run reported here, so reproducibility under the same adapter is preserved.

Integration otherwise follows the minimal recipe:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The ResNet-34 image encoder in the published DiffusionDrive configuration is replaced with the SSL backbone under test (I-JEPA, DINOv2, or MAE; HuggingFace IN-1k or nuScenes-pretrained custom-ViT, per \refsec{sec:meth-backbone-configs}).
    \item The ViT patch-token output is reshaped to a four-stage pyramid compatible with DiffusionDrive's \texttt{MultiScaleCrossAttention} via the adapter in \refsec{sec:meth-feature-proj}. Square (\(224{\times}224\)) and rectangular (\(224{\times}392\)) input geometries are both evaluated.
    \item Both frozen and fully-unfrozen adaptation modes are tested, matching the cross-city benchmark protocol. No partial-thaw runs are included.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Camera-Only Variant}
\label{sec:meth-dd-camera-only}

A camera-only DiffusionDrive variant is included as a stricter probe. LiDAR-dependent inputs and rasterized BEV features are removed; the LiDAR branch is replaced by a fixed learned latent, analogous to the Latent TransFuser construction in~\refsec{sec:bg-transfuser}. The variant targets two readers: the deployment-cost argument for camera-only autonomy, and the stricter test of whether a richer visual representation can partially compensate for the missing geometric channel.

\subsection{Training Configuration}
\label{sec:meth-dd-training}

All DiffusionDrive runs use AdamW with learning rate \(10^{-4}\), weight decay \(10^{-4}\), cosine learning-rate decay with a 3-epoch warmup, and distributed data-parallel training. The anchor bank is fixed at 256 trajectories clustered on \texttt{navtrain}; two denoising steps are used at inference. Where compute permitted, the full DiffusionDrive training schedule is used; some earlier all-city exploratory runs were shortened to widen the backbone sweep, and those rows are flagged in Chapter~\ref{chap:experiments} as architectural probes rather than final leaderboard attempts.

\subsection{What Counts as a Positive Result Under DiffusionDrive}
\label{sec:meth-dd-success}

Because DiffusionDrive's head is actively attenuating the backbone contribution, the evaluation criterion for the generative-planning and cross-city generative studies is stricter than a "top-of-leaderboard" criterion and weaker than a "beat the ResNet baseline outright" criterion. Specifically, the thesis commits, in advance of running the city-disjoint matrix, to the following success condition:

\begin{quote}
\emph{If frozen SSL backbones produce a smaller OOD PDMS drop than supervised ResNet-34 in three or more of the four training-city rows under DiffusionDrive, the representation-centric claim of the thesis is judged to survive the hostile planner. If the signal is inconsistent across training cities, the thesis reports a null result in this study and folds the outcome into the limitations section, rather than re-engineering DiffusionDrive to rescue the effect.}
\end{quote}

\noindent
This pre-registered criterion is what the interpretation of the result tables in \refsec{sec:exp-phase3} anchors on.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:meth-exp-protocol}

Experiments are organized into one exploratory phase followed by four main phases, each building on the previous:

\subsection{Exploratory Lightweight-Head Study}
\label{sec:meth-phase0}

The exploratory study captures the original hypothesis behind the thesis. The initial setup, first documented in~\cite{hamza-sanchez-navsim-report}, used a frozen I-JEPA encoder with a small MLP waypoint regressor over front-camera features and ego state. Later pilot runs expanded this to partial fine-tuning, three-camera fusion, and controlled TransFuser backbone swaps. The point of the study was not to establish a final benchmark number, but to test whether the representation itself was already strong enough to matter when planner capacity was deliberately limited. In the final thesis this study is interpreted qualitatively: it motivated the representation-first view of the problem, but ceased to be the central contribution once Drive-JEPA~\cite{wang-drive-jepa} established that JEPA-style pretraining could already be highly competitive on mixed-city NAVSIM.

\subsection{Cross-City SSL Benchmark}
\label{sec:meth-phase1}

The cross-city benchmark is described in full in~\ref{sec:meth-phase1-cc} (Zero-Shot Cross-City Transfer). This entry exists only to place it in the study sequence: the open-loop LAW pipeline on nuScenes and the closed-loop TransFuser / Latent TransFuser pipeline on NAVSIM, both run under strict city-disjoint splits, together produce the backbone-by-city matrix that Chapter~\ref{chap:experiments} reports.

\subsection{SSL Backbones in Generative Planning}
\label{sec:meth-phase2}

The generative-planning study revisits mixed-city NAVSIM after the appearance of Drive-JEPA. The purpose here is not simply to chase the highest in-distribution PDMS, but to test whether SSL benefits survive in a different generative planning architecture and in a camera-only regime. DiffusionDrive with SSL backbones on the standard NAVSIM split establishes this architectural bridge before any cross-city evaluation. The study covers 9 configurations (Table~\ref{tab:diffusiondrive-allcity}).

\subsection{Cross-City Generative Planning Protocol}
\label{sec:meth-phase3}

The cross-city generative study extends the city-split protocol to DiffusionDrive. The intended comparison is single-city training with DiffusionDrive + I-JEPA versus DiffusionDrive + ResNet-34 across the four NAVSIM cities, yielding eight training runs and a full $4 \times 4$ transfer matrix for each backbone family. This study is methodologically important even when partially complete, because it is the direct test of RQ5: whether the cross-city SSL advantage observed in regression planners persists when the planning head becomes generative.

\subsection{Mechanistic Analysis of Cross-City Representations}
\label{sec:meth-phase4}

The mechanistic analysis asks the question that remains after the earlier studies: why do SSL representations transfer better? This stage uses feature analysis (CKA, linear probing, attention maps, MMD, effective rank) without additional training, drawing on checkpoints from the cross-city benchmark and the generative-planning studies. See Section~\ref{sec:exp-why-ssl} for results.

\section{Metrics}
\label{sec:meth-metrics}

For every experiment, we report:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{PDMS} (overall) and sub-scores: NC, DAC, EP, TTC, Comfort
    \item \textbf{EPDMS} (extended evaluation, where applicable --- DiffusionDrive models)
    \item \textbf{Generalization gap} $\Delta_\text{gen}$ and mean \textbf{OOD PDMS} $S_\text{OOD}$
    \item \textbf{Open-loop transfer inflation} $R_\text{err}$ for L2 displacement and collision rate, as defined in~\cite{naeinian-cross-city-2026}
    \item \textbf{Inference speed} (FPS) for DiffusionDrive variants
    \item All results tracked via Weights \& Biases
\end{itemize}

\section{Implementation Details}
\label{sec:meth-implementation}

Experiments were implemented in PyTorch and PyTorch Lightning, with Hydra-style configuration used to manage backbone, dataset, and optimizer variants. Training was run on the NYU Greene HPC cluster using NVIDIA L40S and H200 GPUs, with distributed data parallelism where required by model size and batch size. Pretrained checkpoints for public SSL backbones were loaded from their released model weights and wrapped through the common adapter interface described above. Experiment orchestration, checkpoint tracking, and evaluation were handled through the local thesis codebase and associated command-line management scripts.

This chapter positions the thesis against the literature it builds on. Chapter~\ref{chap:background} has already introduced end-to-end driving, the NAVSIM benchmark and its scoring, the three SSL paradigms instantiated in this thesis, the four planning architectures used in the experiments, and the motivating framing of geographic domain shift. The present chapter does not restate that material. It instead surveys what adjacent work has attempted and identifies the specific gaps that the six research questions of Section~\ref{sec:research-questions} address. Section~\ref{sec:rw-gaps} consolidates seven gap statements and maps each to one or more research questions.

\section{Self-Supervised Visual Representation Learning}
\label{sec:rw-ssl}

The SSL paradigms relevant to this thesis were introduced in Section~\ref{sec:bg-ssl}. The literature beyond those three representatives spans a larger family of contrastive methods \cite{chen-simclr-2020,he-moco-2020,chen-mocov3-2021,grill-byol-2020,caron-swav-2020,zbontar-barlow-2021,bardes-vicreg-2022}, self-distillation extensions \cite{zhou-ibot-2022}, masked-image-modeling variants \cite{bao-beit-2022,xie-simmim-2022,baevski-data2vec-2022}, and joint-embedding predictive architectures generalized from LeCun's position paper \cite{lecun-ssl-path-2022} to video \cite{bardes-vjepa-2024,assran-vjepa2-2025} and multi-task motion-and-content settings \cite{bardes-mcjepa-2023}. A broader methodological consolidation is provided by the SSL Cookbook \cite{balestriero-ssl-cookbook-2023}.

The empirical question most relevant to this thesis is not which SSL method wins at ImageNet but how their transfer properties differ. Ericsson et al.\ benchmarked thirteen SSL methods across forty downstream tasks and found that leading SSL models match or exceed supervised ImageNet pretraining on most transfer targets, with particular advantage on dense prediction \cite{ericsson-sslbench-2021}. Cole et al.\ qualified this optimism by reporting diminishing returns past 500{,}000 pretraining images, no gain from out-of-domain pretraining data, and greater sensitivity of contrastive SSL to input corruption than supervised baselines \cite{cole-contrastive-2022}. Park et al.\ characterized the complementary inductive biases of contrastive and masked paradigms: contrastive learning emphasizes global shape and late-layer semantics, whereas masked image modeling emphasizes local texture and early-layer features \cite{park-vitsslstudy-2023}. Hendrycks et al.\ showed that auxiliary self-supervised rotation losses improve adversarial, label-noise, and OOD robustness \cite{hendrycks-ssl-robust-2019}, and Grigg et al.\ used CKA to show that intermediate representations converge across SSL and supervised paradigms while final layers diverge sharply \cite{grigg-ssl-cka-2021}. Walmer et al.\ compared supervised, CLIP, MAE, DINO, MoCo, and BEiT ViTs through attention analysis and CKA, identifying offset local attention heads as a supervision-dependent phenomenon \cite{walmer-vit-compare-2023}.

\textbf{Gap.} These studies operate on classification, detection, and segmentation in general-purpose image domains. None systematically compares a joint-embedding SSL (DINOv2), a masked-reconstruction SSL (MAE), and a latent-predictive SSL (I-JEPA) on the same driving planner under matched compute, and none examines what happens to their relative ordering under geographic shift. RQ1 and RQ3 target that gap directly.

\section{Self-Supervised Learning Applied to Autonomous Driving}
\label{sec:rw-ssl-driving}

SSL has been applied extensively to LiDAR and BEV perception but only sparsely to end-to-end planning. On the LiDAR side, PointContrast introduced point-level contrastive objectives on overlapping scans \cite{xie-pointcontrast-2020}. Occupancy-MAE introduced range-aware masked voxel pretraining that roughly halves the label requirements on KITTI-style benchmarks \cite{min-occupancymae-2023}. ALSO used an occupancy-estimation pretext that transfers across SemanticKITTI, nuScenes, SemanticPOSS, and ONCE \cite{boulch-also-2023}. BEV-MAE applied BEV-guided masking to sparse voxel encoders and reached 73.6 NDS on nuScenes under TransFusion-L \cite{lin-bevmae-2024}. UniPAD introduced a universal 3D differentiable-rendering pretext and improved camera NDS by 7.7 points on nuScenes \cite{yang-unipad-2024}. AD-L-JEPA is the first LiDAR JEPA for automotive perception and demonstrates Waymo-to-KITTI transfer with threefold reductions in GPU-hour cost relative to Occupancy-MAE \cite{zhu-adljepa-2025}, and AD-LiST-JEPA extends the formulation to spatiotemporal multi-frame LiDAR \cite{zhu-adlistjepa-2026}. The common feature of this thread is that it stops at perception and reports mAP or NDS rather than planning metrics.

On the camera side, ViDAR used visual point-cloud forecasting as a pretext on nuScenes and improved downstream detection, motion forecasting, and planning when coupled with UniAD \cite{yang-vidar-2024}. GAIA-1 demonstrated that action-conditioned generative world models scale to nine billion parameters on 4{,}700 hours of UK driving data \cite{hu-gaia1-2023}. Juneja et al.\ reported that DINO pretraining outperforms ImageNet-classification pretraining on the CARLA offline leaderboard under a CILRS-style ResNet backbone \cite{juneja-dino-e2e-2024}, and is the closest published precedent for the Phase~1 hypothesis. LAW \cite{li-law-2024}, introduced in Section~\ref{sec:bg-law}, is the most direct camera-side SSL precedent that is also used as a baseline in this thesis.

Drive-JEPA \cite{wang-drive-jepa} is the strongest published result for SSL-initialized planning on NAVSIM, using a V-JEPA~2 video encoder distilled with multi-modal trajectories from OpenScene. Its pretraining spans driving video from Japan, the United States, Singapore, and China, and its evaluation is in-distribution on the pooled \texttt{navtest} split, so it does not isolate the contribution of SSL from the contribution of multi-country pretraining data. Plan-MAE applies masked autoencoding to joint prediction and planning \cite{zhao-planmae-2025}. Poutine combines vision-language-trajectory pretraining with GRPO post-training and reports a zero-shot Japan-to-United-States transfer result on the Waymo Vision-E2E Challenge \cite{poutine-2025}, which is one of the few direct pieces of evidence in the published literature that SSL-style pretraining can preserve planning quality across countries.

\textbf{Gap.} Two structural limitations persist across this literature. First, published SSL-for-driving results routinely mix pretraining geography with downstream evaluation geography, which prevents clean attribution of transfer gains to the SSL objective rather than to data-distribution overlap. Second, when multiple SSL objectives are compared, the comparison is typically confined to one downstream task and one backbone, so the interaction between SSL objective family and the structure of geographic shift remains uncharacterized. RQ1 and RQ3 address the first limitation through a strict city-disjoint protocol. Phase~4 quantifies the underlying representation geometry.

\section{End-to-End Planners on NAVSIM}
\label{sec:rw-navsim-planners}

TransFuser \cite{chitta-transfuser-2023}, Latent TransFuser, LAW \cite{li-law-2024}, and DiffusionDrive \cite{jia-diffusiondrive-2025} were described in Chapter~\ref{chap:background} and serve as the four planners in this thesis. The rest of the NAVSIM planner landscape is organized here by planning-head family, each reviewed only to the extent that it informs the research questions.

\paragraph{Integrated perception-prediction-planning.}
UniAD integrates detection, tracking, mapping, motion forecasting, and occupancy into a unified planning-oriented pipeline and was awarded CVPR~2023 Best Paper \cite{hu-uniad-2023}. VAD \cite{jiang-vad-2023} and VADv2 \cite{chen-vadv2-2024} operate entirely on vectorized scene representations, with VADv2 sampling trajectories from a 4{,}096-anchor planning vocabulary and scoring them probabilistically. PARA-Drive showed that fully parallelizing perception, prediction, and planning matches sequential architectures at roughly the same PDMS while enabling module-level runtime ablation \cite{weng-paradrive-2024}. SparseDrive replaces dense BEV representations with sparse instance-level queries \cite{sun-sparsedrive-2024}, and SparseDriveV2 extends this with scalable trajectory vocabularies and factorized scoring for 92.0 PDMS \cite{zhai-sparsedrivev2-2026}.

\paragraph{Distillation and selection-based planners.}
Hydra-MDP won the CVPR~2024 End-to-End Driving at Scale Challenge by distilling both human and rule-based PDM teachers into a multi-head decoder over a clustered trajectory vocabulary \cite{hydra-mdp-2024}. Hydra-MDP++ extended this with traffic-light and lane-keeping teachers \cite{li-hydramdppp-2025}, and Hydra-NeXt further scaled the encoder and distillation targets \cite{li-hydranext-2025}. DriveSuprim reformulated planning as trajectory selection with coarse-to-fine filtering, reporting 93.5 PDMS on v1 and 87.1 EPDMS on v2 \cite{yuan-drivesuprim-2025}.

\paragraph{Generative trajectory planners.}
GoalFlow replaces diffusion with rectified flow matching conditioned on a scored goal point and reaches 90.3 PDMS with a single denoising step \cite{xing-goalflow-2025}. TransDiffuser reports 94.85 PDMS with decorrelated multi-modal representations and no predefined anchors, though the result has drawn community scrutiny for possible vocabulary overfitting \cite{jiang-transdiffuser-2025}.

\paragraph{World-model and VLM planners.}
WoTE augments a planner with a BEV world-model critic for 87.1 PDMS \cite{li-wote-2025}. ARTEMIS uses a mixture-of-experts head and reports 87.0 PDMS \cite{liu-artemis-2025}. PIE combines Mamba-based fusion with MoE routing for 88.9 PDMS \cite{zhu-pie-2025}. PRIX is a camera-only planner built around a context-aware recalibration transformer, reaching 87.8 PDMS and 84.2 EPDMS without any LiDAR input \cite{trivedi-prix-2025}. PRIX is the most direct published point of comparison for the camera-only configuration tested under RQ4. ReCogDrive combines a vision-language model with reinforcement-learning post-training for 89.6 PDMS \cite{liu-recogdrive-2026}.

\textbf{Gap.} Despite a spread of roughly ten PDMS points across these methods, published NAVSIM results share two structural limitations. First, the methods almost uniformly use a supervised ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-34 image encoder, so reported gains attribute to the planning head, the supervision strategy, or the fusion architecture rather than to the visual representation. Second, because the \texttt{navtest} split pools four cities, no published NAVSIM number isolates the contribution of representation quality under geographic shift. The thesis exploits this gap by fixing the planner and varying only the backbone under a strict city-disjoint protocol, which makes representation quality the only manipulated variable.

\section{Diffusion Models for Trajectory Planning}
\label{sec:rw-diffusion}

Diffusion models \cite{ho-ddpm-2020} reverse a Markovian Gaussian noising process and model multi-modal distributions without mode averaging. DDIM samplers \cite{song-ddim-2021}, classifier-free guidance \cite{ho-cfg-2022}, and consistency models \cite{song-consistency-2023} reduced the number of denoising steps from hundreds to two or fewer, which is what made diffusion tractable for real-time planning in DiffusionDrive (Section~\ref{sec:bg-diffusiondrive}).

Diffusion entered trajectory planning with Diffuser \cite{janner-diffuser-2022}, which denoised entire state-action trajectories with classifier guidance for rewards and inpainting for goals. Diffusion Policy applied conditional DDPMs to visuomotor manipulation and reported a 46.9\% relative improvement over prior behavioral cloning across twelve tasks, precisely because diffusion heads model the multi-modal action distributions that a regression head averages away \cite{chi-diffusion-policy-2023}. MotionDiffuser extended diffusion to multi-agent motion prediction with PCA-compressed trajectory latents and differentiable-cost guidance, and is the closest predecessor to DiffusionDrive \cite{jiang-motiondiffuser-2023}. Anchor-based alternatives include MTR, which introduced learnable motion-query pairs with joint global intention localization and local refinement \cite{shi-mtr-2022}, and MultiPath++, which replaced fixed anchors with learned latent anchors and became a dominant Waymo Open Motion baseline \cite{varadarajan-multipathpp-2022}.

\textbf{Gap.} The 46.9\% Diffusion Policy improvement provides the clearest available evidence that diffusion heads preserve multi-modal structure that regression heads collapse. If that difference matters under geographic shift, SSL backbones should interact asymmetrically with the two head types. No published NAVSIM ablation varies the head under a fixed backbone and a controlled cross-city holdout. RQ5 targets that gap.

\section{Reinforcement-Learning Post-Training for Planning}
\label{sec:rw-rl}

RLHF established the canonical three-stage pipeline of supervised fine-tuning, reward-model learning, and PPO-based policy optimization \cite{ouyang-rlhf-2022}. DPO re-parameterizes preference pairs directly into the policy without an explicit reward model \cite{rafailov-dpo-2023}. GRPO replaces the value function of PPO with group-relative advantages computed within sampled rollouts, which avoids critic instability under sparse metric rewards \cite{grpo-2024}.

Four recent NAVSIM papers import these algorithms into driving. TrajHF combines a multi-conditioned denoiser with GRPO on a PDMS-plus-human-preference reward and reports 93.95 PDMS \cite{li-trajhf-2025}. DriveDPO distills imitation similarity and PDMS into anchor scores and applies iterative trajectory-level DPO with rejected-trajectory mining, reaching 90.0 PDMS with a ResNet-34 backbone \cite{shang-drivedpo-2025}. TISA-DPO introduces a time-invariant spatial-alignment module and a multi-objective DPO variant at 89.8 PDMS \cite{tisadpo-2025}. DiffusionDriveV2 adds scale-adaptive exploration noise and both intra-anchor and inter-anchor truncated GRPO to DiffusionDrive, reaching 91.2 PDMS on v1 and 85.5 EPDMS on v2 \cite{liang-diffusiondrive-v2-2025}. Two complementary threads are relevant. RAD trains an end-to-end policy through large-scale 3DGS-based closed-loop RL and reports a threefold collision-rate reduction on held-out 3DGS environments, which is the closest published analog to an out-of-distribution generalization test for RL-finetuned planners \cite{gao-rad-2025}. CaRL scales a simple route-completion reward to 500~million samples on nuPlan across multiple cities and reaches 91.3 non-reactive and 90.6 reactive Val14 scores \cite{jaeger-carl-2025}. AlphaDrive applies GRPO at the VLM meta-action level with four planning rewards \cite{jiang-alphadrive-2025}.

\textbf{Gap.} None of TrajHF, DriveDPO, TISA-DPO, or DiffusionDriveV2 reports an explicit cross-city evaluation. Each evaluates on the pooled \texttt{navtest} split and relies on in-distribution generalization to report headline numbers. Whether GRPO or DPO gains survive geographic transfer is therefore an open question, and RQ6 treats it as exploratory.

\section{Cross-City and Geographic Generalization}
\label{sec:rw-dg}

The broad domain-generalization literature is surveyed in \cite{wang-domain-gen-survey-2022,zhou-domain-gen-survey-2023}. The most important methodological message from that literature for this thesis is that reported DG improvements are frequently attributable to hyperparameter search budget rather than to method design: DomainBed showed that empirical risk minimization matches or exceeds roughly twenty DG methods on all seven benchmarks under careful model selection \cite{gulrajani-domainbed-2021}. This motivates the choice in this thesis to change only the backbone initialization rather than the DG training procedure. Classical DG techniques for driving, including IRM \cite{arjovsky-irm-2019}, GroupDRO \cite{sagawa-groupdro-2020}, and DANN \cite{ganin-dann-2015}, are therefore referenced for completeness but not used as baselines.

Most driving-specific DG work concentrates on appearance-level shift, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:bg-domain-gen}. Representative methods include ACDC \cite{sakaridis-acdc-2021}, DAFormer \cite{hoyer-daformer-2022}, HRDA \cite{hoyer-hrda-2022}, DACS \cite{tranheden-dacs-2021}, CyCADA \cite{pan-cycada-2018}, and CARLA's train-town/test-town protocol \cite{dosovitskiy-carla-2017}.

Cross-city work has historically targeted perception rather than planning. Chen et al.\ provided the first explicit cross-city UDA benchmark, adapting Cityscapes-trained segmenters to Rome, Rio de Janeiro, Tokyo, and Taipei via static-object priors \cite{chen-crosscity-2017}. Beery et al.\ documented that camera-trap animal-recognition accuracy collapses at new locations because of background and context bias, an early demonstration that geographic generalization is structurally harder than pooled evaluation suggests \cite{beery-cameratrap-2018}. de Vries et al.\ reported substantial accuracy drops for commercial object recognizers on household images from low-income and non-Western countries \cite{devries-geobias-2019}. GeoNet formalized unsupervised adaptation across geographies \cite{kalluri-geonet-2023}. These studies demonstrate that geographic shift produces large and often asymmetric accuracy drops, but none evaluates closed-loop planning consequences.

The planning-level cross-city literature is sparse. VLP reports Boston-to-Singapore and Singapore-to-Boston planning L2 and collision rates, but only as aggregate pairs without analyzing directional asymmetry \cite{pan-vlp-2024}. Trajectory-prediction work including GTG \cite{gtg-2025} and PCM \cite{pcm-2026} pursues city-invariant representations, but at the prediction layer rather than at full end-to-end planning. Naeinian et al.\ \cite{naeinian-cross-city-2026} is the direct precursor to this thesis. It introduces strict city-disjoint evaluation across the four NAVSIM cities for both open-loop nuScenes and closed-loop NAVSIM protocols, and it reports that the choice between supervised and self-supervised backbones measurably changes cross-city behavior while the planner is held fixed. It also documents a directional asymmetry in which Boston-to-Singapore transfer is markedly harder than Singapore-to-Boston, and appears to be the only end-to-end driving work that explicitly quantifies that asymmetry.

\textbf{Gap.} Cross-city transfer at the planning level is essentially undocumented outside the Naeinian et al.\ study. Directional asymmetry is rarely quantified. This thesis extends that protocol to deeper backbone comparisons, additional planners including DiffusionDrive, and the mechanistic analysis of Phase~4.

\section{Mechanistic Analysis of Learned Representations}
\label{sec:rw-mechanistic}

Phase~4 uses a coordinated toolkit to test whether SSL features are geographically more invariant than supervised features at the representation level. Centered Kernel Alignment measures similarity between hidden layers invariant to orthogonal transformation and isotropic scaling, and is the standard tool for cross-model representation comparison \cite{kornblith-cka-2019}. SVCCA is its canonical-correlation predecessor \cite{raghu-svcca-2017}. Linear probing freezes the backbone and trains a linear classifier per layer \cite{alain-linearprobe-2017}. In this thesis it is repurposed as a city-classification probe: the extent to which city identity can be linearly decoded from frozen backbone features provides a direct measure of geographic information leakage into the representation. Maximum Mean Discrepancy provides a non-parametric RKHS distance between feature distributions conditioned on city \cite{gretton-mmd-2012}. Effective rank quantifies representational diversity through the exponential of normalized-singular-value entropy \cite{roy-effrank-2007}, and is used here to test whether SSL backbones maintain higher-dimensional feature subspaces under geographic shift than supervised backbones. Attention-relevance propagation produces class-specific saliency in ViTs through relevance propagation across attention and skip connections \cite{chefer-attention-2021}. Attention rollout \cite{abnar-rollout-2020} and Grad-CAM \cite{selvaraju-gradcam-2017} provide complementary visualizations. Representational Similarity Analysis compares systems through pairwise dissimilarity matrices and bridges neuroscience and deep-network analysis \cite{kriegeskorte-rsa-2008}.

\textbf{Gap.} No prior work in end-to-end driving combines CKA, city-classification linear probing, MMD, effective rank, and attention-relevance visualization on the same set of backbones under a strict cross-city holdout. This combination is what Phase~4 introduces.

\section{Consolidated Gap Analysis}
\label{sec:rw-gaps}

Seven gaps emerge from the literature reviewed above, each supporting one or more research questions.

\begin{description}
    \item[Gap A (RQ1).] \textbf{SSL for driving planning is underexplored.} The SSL-for-driving literature concentrates on LiDAR and BEV perception. Only Drive-JEPA \cite{wang-drive-jepa}, LAW \cite{li-law-2024}, and the earlier Juneja et al.\ study \cite{juneja-dino-e2e-2024} address the planning objective directly with SSL-initialized encoders. None of them conducts a head-to-head comparison among joint-embedding (DINOv2), masked (MAE), and latent-predictive (I-JEPA) objectives on the same planner under matched compute. The thesis closes this gap by fixing LAW, TransFuser, and DiffusionDrive as planners and varying the backbone across the three SSL families and two supervised baselines.
    
    \item[Gap B (RQ1, RQ2, RQ3).] \textbf{Planning-level cross-city transfer is largely undocumented.} Cross-city adaptation has been studied at the perception level since Chen et al.\ \cite{chen-crosscity-2017}, but the planning literature overwhelmingly reports pooled in-distribution PDMS. Naeinian et al.\ \cite{naeinian-cross-city-2026} is the first strictly city-disjoint planning study across the four NAVSIM cities, and this thesis extends that protocol.
    
    \item[Gap C (RQ5).] \textbf{The interaction between planning head and OOD robustness is unquantified.} Published NAVSIM ablations compare heads only under in-distribution evaluation. No prior work varies regression, anchor-scoring, and diffusion heads under a fixed backbone and a controlled cross-city holdout.
    
    \item[Gap D (RQ3, Phase~4).] \textbf{Mechanistic SSL-versus-supervised analyses have not been conducted on driving backbones under geographic shift.} Prior work applied CKA, attention analysis, and shape-bias probes to ImageNet classifiers \cite{walmer-vit-compare-2023,grigg-ssl-cka-2021,park-vitsslstudy-2023}. No prior work applies CKA, city-classification linear probing, MMD between city-conditional feature distributions, and effective-rank analysis to driving backbones under a strict geographic holdout.
    
    \item[Gap E (RQ4).] \textbf{Camera-only SSL plus truncated diffusion is not benchmarked against heavier multi-modal systems.} PRIX \cite{trivedi-prix-2025} demonstrates that a camera-only planner can reach 87.8 PDMS with a specialized recalibration module, and DiffusionDrive uses camera-plus-LiDAR fusion. No published result combines a camera-only I-JEPA backbone with a DiffusionDrive-style truncated diffusion head.
    
    \item[Gap F (RQ6).] \textbf{RL post-training is evaluated in-distribution.} TrajHF \cite{li-trajhf-2025}, DriveDPO \cite{shang-drivedpo-2025}, TISA-DPO \cite{tisadpo-2025}, and DiffusionDriveV2 \cite{liang-diffusiondrive-v2-2025} all evaluate on the pooled \texttt{navtest} split. RAD's 3DGS held-out environments and CaRL's multi-city nuPlan scaling provide partial analogs but not geographic holdouts. Whether GRPO or DPO gains transfer to unseen cities is therefore open.
    
    \item[Gap G (RQ2, Phase~4).] \textbf{Directional asymmetries in cross-city transfer are rarely documented.} The perception literature is aware of asymmetric geographic bias through Beery et al.\ \cite{beery-cameratrap-2018} and de Vries et al.\ \cite{devries-geobias-2019}, but the driving literature typically reports only aggregate pair-wise numbers. The thesis treats directional asymmetry as a first-class outcome variable and connects it to linear-probing results on city classification in Phase~4.
\end{description}

\noindent
These seven gaps define the empirical contribution of the thesis. Phases~0 through~4 provide the mechanism to test each one: Phase~0 establishes that an I-JEPA backbone with a minimal MLP waypoint head is a viable starting point on NAVSIM; Phase~1 varies the backbone under a strict city-disjoint LAW and TransFuser protocol; Phases~2 and~3 integrate a DiffusionDrive truncated-diffusion head with SSL backbones to test the head-representation interaction; and Phase~4 applies CKA, city-classification linear probing, MMD, effective rank, and attention-relevance visualization to test whether the observed generalization gains arise from representation-level geographic invariance rather than from planner expressivity. Together, these phases position the thesis to evaluate, rather than to assume, the central claim that cross-city generalization is a representation problem and that self-supervised pretraining substantially attenuates it.
